Introduction and Scope
Maxwell’s equations determine the electromagnetic field; the field carries energy through the Poynting
vector, momentum through the Maxwell stress tensor, and angular momentum as the moment of
that momentum about a chosen origin [1, 2, 3]. Angular structure is how (E, H) is organized under
rotations of physical space. Azimuthal dependence in the field pattern, angular-momentum density,
and signed angular-momentum transport through a surface answer different questions and must be
kept distinct [4, 3]. A spiral phase factor ejmϕ describes angular organization; net rotational action
on matter is fixed only by the Maxwell stress and its surface integrals [5].

with azimuthal phase ejmϕ, or a near-zone shell where Im(Sc · ˆn) dominates [4, 3]. Energy transfer,
linear momentum transfer, and—when symmetry permits—angular momentum transfer are fixed by

1.0.1
Historical lineage of field energy, momentum, and angular structure
Faraday’s experiments on induction and magnetism forced a shift from action at a distance to
fields as the primary carriers of electric and magnetic action in space [1]. Maxwell’s 1865 paper
and the 1873 Treatise supplied the mathematical field pair (E, H), the displacement current that
closes Ampère’s law in time-dependent form, and the wave equation that predicted electromagnetic
propagation at the speed of light [1]. Once the field itself became dynamical, two questions followed
immediately: where does electromagnetic energy reside, and how does the field exchange linear and
rotational mechanical action with matter?
Maxwell answered the mechanical side within the same field picture. His stress tensor relates
bilinear products of (E, H) to force and momentum density in the electromagnetic medium [1, 4].
Poynting [2] then identified energy transport with the flux s = E × H, giving a local account of
power crossing a surface. Hertz’s experiments (1887–1888) showed that Maxwell’s predicted waves

could be generated and detected in the laboratory, converting the wave equation from theory into
measurable radiation [4]. Radiation-pressure measurements on absorbing and reflecting surfaces
confirmed that the same field carries directed momentum as well as energy; on a perfect absorber
the pressure is pabs = |⟨S⟩|/c, on a perfect reflector prefl = 2|⟨S⟩|/c in vacuum [4].
The early twentieth century did not introduce electromagnetic stress—Maxwell had already done
so—but reopened the question of momentum inside material media. Abraham [8] and Minkowski [9]
proposed different symmetric stress–energy forms for polarizable matter; their disagreement concerns
how much momentum is stored in a dielectric, not whether vacuum radiation exerts pressure. In
source-free exterior regions both reduce to the vacuum Maxwell stress used throughout this chapter
[4].
Stratton [4], Sommerfeld [10], Harrington [6], and Jackson [11] consolidated the mathematical
apparatus of boundary-value radiation problems: impressed sources, outgoing conditions, dyadic
Green functions, and surface flux integrals for power and momentum. Chu [7] emphasized that
finite source size and stored reactive energy bound what a compact antenna can radiate, so far-zone
patterns cannot be separated from near-zone storage. Tai [12] extended the same field-theoretic
language to helicity and dual symmetry in monochromatic media.
Rotational action entered experimentally with Beth [3], who measured the mechanical torque on
a quartz wave plate in circularly polarized light. The observation confirmed that angular momentum
is transported by the Maxwell field and delivered to matter through stress at an interface, not
inferred from phase portrait alone. Belinfante [13] clarified that canonical and kinetic momentum
densities differ by the divergence of a spin current; correspondingly, local “orbital” and “spin” labels
partition total angular momentum in a convention-dependent way, while closed-surface flux integrals
remain anchored to (E, H) [13, 4].
Allen et al. [5] later gave paraxial beams a countable azimuthal index m through the phase
exp(jmϕ). That vocabulary made helical phase structure experimentally accessible, but the mea-
surable quantities of this chapter remain the Poynting flux, Maxwell traction, and angular flux
⟨M⟩= r × ⟨T⟩on a radiative shell—quantities whose history runs from Maxwell and Poynting
through Beth to the present chapter [2, 3, 5].
1.0.2
Source data, boundaries, and Maxwell consistency
Every flux integral in this chapter refers to a Maxwell field pair (E, H) fixed by impressed sources,
constitutive law, boundary data, and—when radiation is claimed—an outgoing condition [4, 1]. A
tangential field prescribed tangential pattern on an aperture is not, by itself, such a specification [6].
On a PEC-backed circular aperture, a charge-continuous impressed current Jimp closes Maxwell’s
equations and defines radiated flux on ΓR; prescribing Et alone on the rim without a matching
source may satisfy a local boundary relation yet fail to tie Poynting and torque integrals to any
synthesizable excitation [4, 6]. Figure 1.4 contrasts the two configurations. Stratton [4] fixed
the physical content of a radiation problem: spatial domain Ω, boundary Γ, constitutive data
χ, impressed sources (Jimp, ρ), and harmonic frequency under the exp(+jωt) convention adopted
throughout this volume.
Given impressed current and charge, together with the constitutive medium, boundary conditions
on Γ = ∂Ω, and angular frequency ω > 0 in the exp(+jωt) convention, Maxwell’s equations determine
the electromagnetic field once the appropriate radiation condition is imposed [1, 4]. An angular
claim is meaningful only after that boundary-value problem is closed.
Inconsistent boundary traces cannot be repaired by deeper harmonic listing. The source and
boundary conditions precede every transport statement [4].

Rotate a vector by angle α about axis ˆu: v′ = Rαv.
A second-order tensor transforms as

If the impressed current on a feed is rotated through α while the horn, ground plane, and medium
share the same axisymmetry, does the radiated field rotate with it? When source, medium, and
boundaries share rotational symmetry, solving Maxwell’s equations after rotating Jimp yields the
same physical field as rotating the solution [4, 14]. An anisotropic radome or azimuthally varying ε

An anisotropic radome, an azimuthally varying permittivity, or a source that does not share the

not track the physical rotation of the feed, even though total radiated power on ΓR may remain
unchanged [6]. Rotational symmetry of Maxwell radiation is therefore a property of the source,
medium, and boundary geometry together—not of a phase portrait viewed in isolation [4].
1.0.4
Assumptions, finite energy, and domain of validity
A coax-fed pyramidal horn over a ground plane illustrates how an impressed current on a finite
feed support, together with conducting boundaries and an outgoing radiation condition, defines a
Maxwell boundary-value problem [4]. The next question is whether the resulting field pair has finite
energy on the control volume on which flux and torque will be integrated [1].

law is evaluated—not a separate procedural gate imposed before transport is discussed.

1.1
Maxwell Theory and Conservation Laws
Charge continuity follows from the divergence of Ampère’s law. Energy and momentum follow from
the curl structure of (E, H). Angular momentum is the moment of electromagnetic momentum
carried by the Maxwell stress tensor [1, 2, 3]. Maxwell’s equations in harmonic form are
∇× E = −jωµ0H,
∇× H = Jimp + jωε0E,
(1.5)
together with the divergence constraints and boundary completion of section 1.0 [4].
Helicity sectors, modal coefficients, and orbital labels are coordinate listings on radiative fields,
not independent degrees of freedom that override curl–divergence constraints [1, 11]. Assigning
azimuthal phase to E alone without updating H through ∇× E = −jωµ0H violates local Maxwell
compatibility [4].
1.1.1
Maxwell equations: differential, integral, vacuum constitutive relations
Maxwell’s equations couple E and H at every point: Faraday’s law links ∇× E to H, and Ampère–
Maxwell law links ∇× H to E and the impressed sources [1, 4]. Angular structure is always a
property of the coupled (E, H) state—never of a single component viewed in isolation [4].
Three equivalent formulations answer three distinct physical questions. The differential form
states local field relations at each point. The integral form states the same laws on finite surfaces
and volumes—the form in which enclosed charge, circulating E, and power crossing a shell are
evaluated. The vacuum constitutive relations link (E, H) to (D, B) and fix the propagation scales c,
η0, and k0 [4].
Figure 1.6 displays the vacuum constitutive link: ε0 and µ0 map (E, H) to (D, B), fix wave
speed c and impedance η0, and set the wavenumber k0 quoted in every later phase gradient and
shell radius kr. Changing E without updating H through this closure violates Faraday’s law and
breaks propagation consistency [1, 4].

1.1.3
Sources, boundary conditions, and the Maxwell boundary-value problem
No radiating structure exists without a feed and an outer world. Maxwell’s local equations, stated in
Subsection 1.1.1, govern how (E, H) must behave pointwise; they do not, by themselves, determine
which impressed currents, interface jumps, and outer-boundary data correspond to a physically
realizable field [4]. The question that now arises is therefore immediate: given a tangential field on
an aperture, does a finite impressed source, compatible interface data, and outgoing selection exist
such that flux integrals on ΓR name the same physics as the Maxwell field launched by that source?
Prescribing tangential Et on an aperture is not equivalent to specifying a source. Harrington [6]
and Chu [7] showed repeatedly that finite extent and material boundaries restrict what tangential
patterns can be synthesized. A Maxwell field is determined by the sources that produce it, the
constitutive properties of the medium, and the boundary conditions imposed by the surrounding
matter [1].

1.2
Angular Structure of Electromagnetic Fields
Angular structure is the organization of the Maxwell field under rotations of physical space: spatial
phase structure, polarization structure, directional radiation structure, angular-momentum density,
and angular-momentum flux are distinct physical features [13, 2]. Azimuthal dependence exp(jmϕ)
describes angular organization of the field; its relation to angular-momentum transport follows
only under the separable or paraxial conditions developed below [5, 4]. Signed surface integrals of
⟨M⟩= r × ⟨T⟩decide radiated rotational action [3].
1.2.1
Rotating source versus rotating pattern

Gauge freedom and angular structure
Rotation and origin declaration fixed which mechanical reports built from (E, H) are objective.
Potentials introduce a further question that rotation alone does not settle: can (A, ϕ) be changed

1.0.1 Historical lineage of field energy, momentum, and angular structure
Faraday’s experiments on induction and magnetism forced a shift from action at a distance
tofieldsas the primary carriers of electric and magnetic action in space [3]. Maxwell’s 1865
paper and the 1873Treatisesupplied the mathematical field pair(E , H), the displacement
current that closes Ampère’s law in time-dependent form, and the wave equation that predicted
electromagnetic propagation at the speed of light [3]. Once the field itself became dynamical,
two questions followed immediately: where does electromagnetic energy reside, and how does
the field exchange linear and rotational mechanical action with matter?
Maxwell answered the mechanical side within the same field picture. His stress tensor relates
bilinear products of(E, H)to force and momentum density in the electromagnetic medium
[3, 2]. Poynting [4] then identified energy transport with the fluxs=E× H, giving a local
account of power crossing a surface. Hertz’s experiments (1887–1888) showed that Maxwell’s
predicted waves could be generated and detected in the laboratory, converting the wave equation
from theory into measurable radiation [2]. Radiation-pressure measurements on absorbing and
reflecting surfaces confirmed that the same field carries directed momentum as well as energy; on
a perfect absorber the pressure ispabs =|⟨S⟩|/c, on a perfect reflectorprefl = 2|⟨S⟩|/c in vacuum
[2].
The early twentieth century did notintroduceelectromagnetic stress—Maxwell had already
done so—but reopened the question of momentuminsidematerial media. Abraham [8] and
Minkowski [9] proposed different symmetric stress–energy forms for polarizable matter; their
disagreement concerns how much momentum is stored in a dielectric, not whether vacuum
radiation exerts pressure. In source-free exterior regions both reduce to the vacuum Maxwell
stress used throughout this chapter [2].

Rotational action entered experimentally with Beth [1], who measured the mechanical torque
on a quartz wave plate in circularly polarized light. The observation confirmed that angular
momentum is transported by the Maxwell field and delivered to matter through stress at an
interface, not inferred from phase portrait alone. Belinfante [13] clarified that canonical and
kinetic momentum densities differ by the divergence of a spin current; correspondingly, local
“orbital” and “spin” labels partition total angular momentum in a convention-dependent way,
while closed-surface flux integrals remain anchored to(E,H)[13, 2].
Allenet al.[ 5] later gave paraxial beams a countable azimuthal indexm through the phase
exp(jmϕ). That vocabulary made helical phase structure experimentally accessible, but the
measurable quantities of this chapter remain the Poynting flux, Maxwell traction, and angular
flux⟨M⟩ =r ×⟨ T⟩ on a radiative shell—quantities whose history runs from Maxwell and
Poynting through Beth to the present chapter [4, 1, 5].
1.0.2 Source data, boundaries, and Maxwell consistency
Every flux integral in this chapter refers to a Maxwell field pair(E, H)fixed by impressed
sources, constitutive law, boundary data, and—when radiation is claimed—an outgoing condition
[2, 3]. A tangential field prescribed tangential pattern on an aperture is not, by itself, such a
specification [6].
On a PEC-backed circular aperture, a charge-continuous impressed currentJimp closes
Maxwell’s equations and defines radiated flux onΓR; prescribingEt alone on the rim without
a matching source may satisfy a local boundary relation yet fail to tie Poynting and torque
integrals to any synthesizable excitation [2, 6]. Figure 1.4 contrasts the two configurations.
Stratton [2] fixed the physical content of a radiation problem: spatial domainΩ, boundaryΓ,
constitutive dataχ, impressed sources(Jimp,ρ ), and harmonic frequency under theexp(+jωt)
convention adopted throughout this volume.
Given impressed current and charge, together with the constitutive medium, boundary
conditions onΓ = ∂Ω, and angular frequencyω >0in the exp(+jωt) convention, Maxwell’s
equations determine the electromagnetic field once the appropriate radiation condition is imposed
[3, 2]. An angular claim is meaningful only after that boundary-value problem is closed.

1.1.1 Maxwell equations: differential, integral, vacuum constitutive relations
A time-varying electric field cannot remain an isolated scalar pattern in space: Faraday’s law ties
its curl toH, and Ampère–Maxwell ties the curl ofHback toEand to the sources that drive
the system [3, 2]. Specifying only the spatial phase of one Cartesian component—for example
a forward exp(−jkz) factor onEx—does not yet define an electromagnetic solution until the
pairedHsatisfies both curl equations and the divergence constraints on the same domain [2, 11].
The local Maxwell system is therefore the starting point for every later transport, flux, and
angular-momentum statement in this chapter [2].
Harmonic analysis uses the time factorexp(+jωt) in a piecewise smooth regionΩ⊂R 3 with
boundaryΓ = ∂Ω[ 2]. The phasorsE(r)andH(r)then satisfy ∂/∂t→ +jω. Impressed current
densityJ imp(r)and charge densityρ(r)enter Ampère–Maxwell and Gauss’s electric law; vacuum
permittivity ε0 and permeabilityµ0 are SI constants linking field intensities to flux densities
[2, 6]. Three complementary presentations of the same physics will be used: thedifferential

1.3 Rotational covariance and reference frames
Flux integrals in section 1.2 are evaluated in a fixed reference frame with fixed origin. If the
source is rotated while laboratory axes remain fixed, the field is physically rotated only when the
source, medium, and boundaries share the corresponding rotational symmetry [2, 14]. Relabeling
coordinate axes while the impressed current stays fixed changes component charts but not the
source. Vectors, the Maxwell stress tensor, and traction then transform as physical objects under
rotation; gauge relabeling of potentials is a separate non-uniqueness treated in section 1.4 [13, 3].

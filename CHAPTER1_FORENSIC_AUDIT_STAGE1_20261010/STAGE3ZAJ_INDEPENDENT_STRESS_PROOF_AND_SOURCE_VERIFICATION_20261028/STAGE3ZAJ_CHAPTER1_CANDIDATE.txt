1.0
Introduction and Scope
Suppose a narrow circularly polarized beam falls on a torsion-mounted wave plate: the plate can
rotate even when the intensity pattern on a transverse screen looks featureless about the beam
axis [1]. The mechanical response is not read from a photograph of phase contours; it is the
integrated Maxwell stress on the material boundary. That experiment motivates the distinction
pursued in this chapter: what the field looks like in angle, what angular momentum it stores
locally, and what signed angular momentum it transports outward through a closed surface are
three different questions [2, 1].
Maxwell’s equations determine (E, H); the same pair carries energy through the Poynting
vector, linear momentum through the Maxwell stress tensor, and angular momentum as the
moment of that momentum about a chosen origin [3, 4]. Angular structure is how (E, H)
is organized under rotations of physical space. A spiral phase factor ejmϕ labels azimuthal
organization of a transverse pattern; net torque on matter follows only from ⟨T⟩, traction
t = ⟨T⟩· ˆn, and the stress-moment integrals of Subsection 1.1.6 [5, 2].
Consider next a harmonic field on a material interface, a transverse mode with ejmϕ, or a
near-zone shell where Im(Sc · ˆn) dominates local normal exchange [2]. Active power crossing a
surface element is fixed by Re(Sc · ˆn) dS; mechanical exchange is fixed by ⟨T⟩and ⟨M⟩= r×⟨T⟩.

1.0.1
Historical lineage of field energy, momentum, and angular structure
Faraday’s experiments on induction and magnetism forced a shift from action at a distance
to fields as the primary carriers of electric and magnetic action in space [3]. Maxwell’s 1865
paper and the 1873 Treatise supplied the mathematical field pair (E, H), the displacement
current that closes Ampère’s law in time-dependent form, and the wave equation that predicted
electromagnetic propagation at the speed of light [3]. Once the field itself became dynamical,
two questions followed immediately: where does electromagnetic energy reside, and how does
the field exchange linear and rotational mechanical action with matter?
Maxwell answered the mechanical side within the same field picture. His stress tensor relates
bilinear products of (E, H) to force and momentum density in the electromagnetic medium
[3, 2]. Poynting [4] then identified energy transport with the flux s = E × H, giving a local
account of power crossing a surface. Hertz’s experiments (1887–1888) showed that Maxwell’s
predicted waves could be generated and detected in the laboratory, converting the wave equation
from theory into measurable radiation [2]. Radiation-pressure measurements on absorbing and
reflecting surfaces confirmed that the same field carries directed momentum as well as energy; on
a perfect absorber the pressure is pabs = |⟨S⟩|/c, on a perfect reflector prefl = 2|⟨S⟩|/c in vacuum
[2].
The early twentieth century did not introduce electromagnetic stress—Maxwell had already
done so—but reopened the question of momentum inside material media. Abraham [8] and
Minkowski [9] proposed different symmetric stress–energy forms for polarizable matter; their
disagreement concerns how much momentum is stored in a dielectric, not whether vacuum
radiation exerts pressure. In source-free exterior regions both reduce to the vacuum Maxwell
stress used throughout this chapter [2].

Rotational action entered experimentally with Beth [1], who measured the mechanical torque
on a quartz wave plate in circularly polarized light. The observation confirmed that angular
momentum is transported by the Maxwell field and delivered to matter through stress at an
interface, not inferred from phase portrait alone. Belinfante [13] clarified that canonical and
kinetic momentum densities differ by the divergence of a spin current; correspondingly, local
“orbital” and “spin” labels partition total angular momentum in a convention-dependent way,
while closed-surface flux integrals remain anchored to (E, H) [13, 2].
Allen et al. [5] later gave paraxial beams a countable azimuthal index m through the phase
exp(jmϕ). That vocabulary made helical phase structure experimentally accessible, but the
measurable quantities of this chapter remain the Poynting flux, Maxwell traction, and angular
flux ⟨M⟩= r × ⟨T⟩on a radiative shell—quantities whose history runs from Maxwell and
Poynting through Beth to the present chapter [4, 1, 5].
1.0.2
Source data, boundaries, and Maxwell consistency
Every flux integral in this chapter refers to a Maxwell field pair (E, H) fixed by impressed
sources, constitutive law, boundary data, and—when radiation is claimed—an outgoing condition
[2, 3]. A tangential field prescribed tangential pattern on an aperture is not, by itself, such a
specification [6].
On a PEC-backed circular aperture, a charge-continuous impressed current Jimp closes
Maxwell’s equations and defines radiated flux on ΓR; prescribing Et alone on the rim without
a matching source may satisfy a local boundary relation yet fail to tie Poynting and torque
integrals to any synthesizable excitation [2, 6]. Figure 1.4 contrasts the two configurations.
Stratton [2] fixed the physical content of a radiation problem: spatial domain Ω, boundary Γ,
constitutive data χ, impressed sources (Jimp, ρ), and harmonic frequency under the exp(+jωt)
convention adopted throughout this volume.
Given impressed current and charge, together with the constitutive medium, boundary
conditions on Γ = ∂Ω, and angular frequency ω > 0 in the exp(+jωt) convention, Maxwell’s
equations determine the electromagnetic field once the appropriate radiation condition is imposed
[3, 2]. An angular claim is meaningful only after that boundary-value problem is closed.

1.0.3
Rotation and Maxwell compatibility
Rotate a vector by angle α about axis ˆu: v′ = Rαv. A second-order tensor transforms as
T ′
ij = RikTklRjl, or T′ = RTR⊤in dyadic notation [2]. A vector field transforms actively as
(TRF)(r) = R F(R−1r): the source is rotated in space, not merely relabelled [14].
If the impressed current on a feed is rotated through α while the horn, ground plane, and
medium share the same axisymmetry, does the radiated field rotate with it? When source,
medium, and boundaries share rotational symmetry, solving Maxwell’s equations after rotating
Jimp yields the same physical field as rotating the solution [2, 14]. An anisotropic radome or
azimuthally varying ε breaks that equivalence only when the full constitutive and boundary
configuration fails to share the same rotation symmetry about the axis in question [2].
1.0.4
Assumptions, finite energy, and domain of validity
A coax-fed pyramidal horn over a ground plane illustrates how an impressed current on a finite
feed support, together with conducting boundaries and an outgoing radiation condition, defines
a Maxwell boundary-value problem [2]. Before Poynting, stress, or angular-flux integrals are
interpreted, one must know whether the underlying field pair is regular enough on the chosen
control volume for those quadratic flux densities to have finite surface integrals [3, 4, 2].
Physical stored energy and mathematical field integrability are related but not identical.
Under the harmonic convention exp(+jωt), the cycle-averaged electromagnetic energy density in
a linear, isotropic, lossless medium with real, frequency-independent ε and µ at the operating
frequency is u = 1
4(ε|E|2 + µ|H|2) [2]. In dispersive or dissipative media the same phasor
magnitudes do not, by themselves, define a unique local stored-energy density; dissipation
appears in Im(ε) and Im(µ) through the complex Poynting balance of Subsection 1.1.5 [2, 6]. In
vacuum, ε = ε0 and µ = µ0, so u has SI units J m−3. By contrast, |E|2 alone carries units V2m−2
and |H|2 carries A2m−2; adding them without constitutive weights is not a single physical density.
The admissibility condition used here is therefore a weighted field norm on a specified bounded
volume V : Eq. (1.4) certifies integrability of ε0|E|2 + µ0|H|2 over V . It does not, without further
trace or smoothness hypotheses, certify classical boundary values on ∂V or the finiteness of
every quadratic Poynting or stress flux on ∂V [2].

1.1.1
Maxwell equations: differential, integral, vacuum constitutive relations
A time-varying electric field cannot remain an isolated scalar pattern in space: Faraday’s law ties
its curl to H, and Ampère–Maxwell ties the curl of H back to E and to the sources that drive
the system [3, 2]. Specifying only the spatial phase of one Cartesian component—for example
a forward exp(−jkz) factor on Ex—does not yet define an electromagnetic solution until the
paired H satisfies both curl equations and the divergence constraints on the same domain [2, 11].
The local Maxwell system is therefore the starting point for every later transport, flux, and
angular-momentum statement in this chapter [2].
Harmonic analysis uses the time factor exp(+jωt) in a piecewise smooth region Ω⊂R3 with
boundary Γ = ∂Ω[2]. The phasors E(r) and H(r) then satisfy ∂/∂t →+jω. Impressed current
density Jimp(r) and charge density ρ(r) enter Ampère–Maxwell and Gauss’s electric law; vacuum
permittivity ε0 and permeability µ0 are SI constants linking field intensities to flux densities
[2, 6]. Three complementary presentations of the same physics will be used: the differential
form for pointwise coupling, the integral form for finite control surfaces and volumes, and the
vacuum constitutive relations that close the system in free space and fix propagation scales [2].
Figure 1.6 summarizes how ε0 and µ0 connect (E, H) to (D, B) and how the same constants
enter wave speed, impedance, and wavenumber in later formulas [3, 2]. Any update to E that is
not propagated to H through Faraday’s law breaks that closure and cannot represent a Maxwell
field.

1.1.2
From inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation to outgoing Green function
The local Maxwell system of Subsection 1.1.1 fixes how E and H must balance at each point
for prescribed Jimp and ρ, but it does not yet supply a constructive map from a finite source
distribution to the field at a distant observation point [2, 3]. That map is the classical Green-
function problem: given impressed data on compact support, determine the outgoing harmonic
field that satisfies the radiation condition at infinity [2, 11].

domain response, because e+jω(t−R/c) = e+jωt e−jk0R and k0 = ω/c [2]. The minus sign in e−jk0R
selects the outgoing spherical wave that carries energy toward increasing R under this time

1.3
Rotational covariance and reference frames
Flux integrals in section 1.2 are evaluated in a fixed reference frame with fixed origin. If the

source, medium, and boundaries share the corresponding rotational symmetry [2, 14]. Relabeling
coordinate axes while the impressed current stays fixed changes component charts but not the
source. Vectors, the Maxwell stress tensor, and traction then transform as physical objects under
rotation; gauge relabeling of potentials is a separate non-uniqueness treated in section 1.4 [13, 3].

Summary
Within the harmonic Maxwell class developed here, compatible impressed sources, interface

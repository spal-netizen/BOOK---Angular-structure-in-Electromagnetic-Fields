The schematic above maps the logical order of \ref{ch:01}: each downward step is stricter than the one above it, and the side annotations remind us that geometry, aperture, bandwidth, and dynamic range are not afterthoughts but ceilings on every legitimate angular report \cite{maxwell1873}.

Four questions, each stricter than the last, follow Figure~\ref{fig:ch1.master-philosophy}.
First: does \((\E,\H)\) satisfy Maxwell's equations with consistent sources and
boundaries on the declared domain? Second: can a current distribution of finite
support actually produce the observed pattern---that is, can a laboratory source
of bounded extent generate the same far-zone fields?
Third: can a finite-aperture instrument recover the pattern within its rank and
bandwidth? Fourth: does the pattern export qualified angular momentum flux through
the chosen control surface \(\Gamma\)?
The four questions are cumulative: a positive answer at a lower step is prerequisite for any claim at a higher step; skipping a step is how catalogue templates masquerade as measured angular structure \cite{stratton1941}.
\phantomsection\label{prin:ch1.rep-physics}\label{def:ch1.hierarchy-classes}\label{hier:ch1.angular-status}

Compact-range practice makes the four-question ladder tangible. A horn feed at $10\,\mathrm{GHz}$ may satisfy Maxwell on the mesh yet fail synthesizable if the impressed current is replaced by a tangential-field template copied from a catalogue. A six-probe scan may recover main-beam power yet miss sidelobe structure the aperture actually radiates. A reactive near-field pattern may show vivid phase circulation while the outward flux integral on $\Gamma_R$ remains below the wattmeter noise floor. Torque and angular-flux reports therefore arrive last---only after geometry, source specification, and instrument rank have already fixed what can be produced and observed \cite{stratton1941}.

Consider a PEC-backed circular aperture of radius $a=5\,\mathrm{mm}$ at $f=30\,\mathrm{GHz}$, the scale of a compact-range calibration target \cite{stratton1941}. Two numerical models may share the same tangential $\E_t$ on the rim: Model~A supplies a charge-continuous impressed current $\Jimp$ on the feed support; Model~B prescribes $\E_t$ alone with no extension to $\Jimp$ \cite{harrington2001}. Model~A can in principle pass all four $\mathcal{D}_J=1$ questions---Maxwell closure, finite-source range membership, finite-aperture wattmeter reading, and qualified angular transport---because a synthesizable source exists \cite{stratton1941}. Model~B may still satisfy a differential Maxwell solve on a truncated domain yet fail $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{real}}$: without a matched source model, $\oint_{\Gamma_R}$ closure and transport claims remain undefined, and flux integrals on $\partial V$ do not name laboratory quantities \cite{stratton1941}.

When all four questions are posed in order, a field that exports measurable angular
flux must already satisfy Maxwell's equations on the declared domain, be producible
by a current distribution of finite support, lie within the recoverable rank of a
finite-aperture instrument, and carry qualified angular-momentum flux through the
chosen control surface \cite{stratton1941}.

\subsection{Historical lineage of field energy, momentum, and angular structure}
\label{sec:ch01.0.historical-lineage}

Electromagnetic angular structure did not appear as a single postulate. It accumulated
as successive generations clarified how energy, linear momentum, and rotational action
are carried by \((\E,\H)\) and exchanged with matter. Maxwell \cite{maxwell1873}
unified electric and magnetic phenomena through the local field pair and the
displacement current that closes Amp\`ere's law in time-dependent form. Once fields
were treated as dynamical objects rather than as auxiliary potentials, the question
of how power and mechanical action propagate through space became unavoidable.

Poynting \cite{poynting1884} answered the energetic part with the flux vector
\(\mathbf{S}=\E\times\H\), which identifies outward power transport through a
surface in the harmonic regime developed in Section~\ref{sec:ch01.1.4}. Abraham
\cite{abraham1910} and Minkowski \cite{minkowski1908} extended the same field
picture to linear momentum density and stress, establishing that radiation pressure
and recoil are field-mechanical effects rather than properties of distant charges
alone. Stratton \cite{stratton1941} synthesized these threads into the modern treatise
form still used in antenna and scattering theory. His Maxwell system, uniqueness
theorems, and stress-tensor treatment fixed the operational sequence---specify
geometry, sources, and boundaries; solve for \((\E,\H)\); integrate flux through
declared surfaces---that every later angular argument inherits. Chu \cite{chu1948}
added the finite-size viewpoint: stored reactive energy and antenna quality factor
bound what a compact structure can radiate, so far-zone organization is never
independent of source extent and bandwidth. Harrington \cite{harrington2001} and
Tai \cite{tai1994} carried that synthesis into microwave engineering, separating
admissible field templates from what finite feeds and apertures can synthesize.
Belinfante \cite{belinfante1940} and Beth \cite{beth1936} supplied the rotational
closure. Belinfante's symmetric stress tensor and spin-density redistribution
explained why local angular-momentum bookkeeping can be gauge-sensitive while
surface flux integrals remain meaningful. Beth's torsion-balance experiment showed
that circularly polarized microwave radiation transfers measurable mechanical
torque, linking Poynting flux to laboratory mechanics. Allen \emph{et al.}
\cite{allen1992} later made azimuthal phase structure experimentally accessible in
paraxial optics; their Laguerre--Gaussian modes provided a compact modal vocabulary
without replacing the Maxwellian flux pair \((\Pi,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J)\) defined in
Section~\ref{sec:ch01.2.1}.

That historical arc explains why Chapter~1 does not begin with modal indices or beam names: energy, momentum, and rotational action were established as field properties long before modern OAM vocabulary, and every angular descriptor used later in the book must remain accountable to the same flux and torque chain \cite{poynting1884}.

\subsection{Rotation and Maxwell compatibility}
\label{sec:ch01.0.2}

On a turntable compact range, rotating the antenna under test is the everyday check that a pattern rotates rigidly with the feed while total radiated power stays fixed \cite{hansen1988}. That experiment presupposes a deeper statement: when the source region and surrounding medium share rotational symmetry, rotating the excitation should rotate the radiated field in the same way---a requirement Stratton \cite{stratton1941} enforced long before any modal index was named. Violations usually signal anisotropic boundaries, mismatched material data, or a source model that does not close charge continuity. The commutator test below makes that laboratory expectation precise.

For rotation \(R_\alpha\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\) with induced action \(\mathcal{R}_\alpha\) on sources and fields, Maxwell compatibility requires that rotation commute with the field operator \(\mathcal{M}_{\Omega,\Gamma,\chi}\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.rotation-commutator-criterion}
\left[\mathcal{R}_\alpha,\mathcal{M}_{\Omega,\Gamma,\chi}\right]\Jimp
=\mathbf{0}
\quad\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_\alpha.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{I}_\alpha$ is the admissible interval of rotation angles for which the symmetry operation is defined on the declared domain---typically a full $2\pi$ azimuth on an axisymmetric range, or a finite turntable arc when mechanical stops apply \cite{hansen1988}. The operator $\mathcal{R}_\alpha$ rotates impressed current components and spatial coordinates consistently; $\mathcal{M}_{\Omega,\Gamma,\chi}$ is the Maxwell field operator fixed by geometry $\Omega$, boundary class $\Gamma$, and constitutive data $\chi$ \cite{stratton1941}. When the commutator vanishes, a pattern rolled through $\alpha$ on the stage is the same physical export seen in a rotated coordinate frame; when it does not, angular labels assigned in harmonic charts will scramble even though a wattmeter on $\Gamma_R$ may still read the same total power \cite{harrington2001}.

When that commutator does not vanish, rotation scrambles angular content established at the feed. Section~\ref{sec:ch01.3.1} quantifies the effect through the ratio
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.rotation-mismatch-index}
\kappa_{\mathrm{rot}}(\alpha)
=
\frac{
\left\|
\left[\mathcal{R}_\alpha,\mathcal{M}_{\Omega,\Gamma,\chi}\right]\Jimp
\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_F}
}{
\left\|
\mathcal{M}_{\Omega,\Gamma,\chi}\Jimp
\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_F}
+\epsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}
},
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_F}$ is the field energy norm on the analysis domain---the same $L^2$ measure of $(\E,\H)$ used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.energy-class}---and $\epsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}>0$ is a fixed regularizer that prevents division by zero when the impressed excitation is null \cite{stratton1941}. Large $\kappa_{\mathrm{rot}}$ means rotation no longer relabels the export cleanly: $|m=1|$ power sectors mix under anisotropic radomes or wedge dielectrics, and single-cut azimuth scans cannot validate coefficient rotation laws \cite{hansen1988}. On the range, $\kappa_{\mathrm{rot}}$ rises when a lossy anisotropic radome is inserted between a center-fed dipole and the scan sphere---the feed rotation and the reported phase pattern cease to close on the same $D^{(1)}$ block \cite{stratton1941}. Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ch1.rotation-commutator-criterion} and~\eqref{eq:ch1.rotation-mismatch-index} are operational: rotation is a symmetry of Maxwell export only when the operator commutes with the impressed source action on the declared domain \cite{stratton1941}. Section~\ref{sec:ch01.3} develops the full rotation algebra for vectors, tensors, and dyads; note the laboratory warning---misaligned radomes and anisotropic windows scramble angular labels while total power on $\Gamma_R$ may remain unchanged \cite{hansen1988}. Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.0.3} next fixes which field pairs may enter conservation integrals at all \cite{stratton1941}.

\subsection{Assumptions, finite-energy admissibility, and domain of validity}
\label{sec:ch01.0.3}

% HARRINGTON-OPEN:sec:ch01.0.3
A coax-fed pyramidal horn over a ground plane defines the impressed-current and boundary-data tuple technicians declare before Maxwell export is computed \cite{stratton1941}. Maxwell's equations on the declared exterior domain remain the governing constraint \cite{maxwell1873}.

Transport claims require fields integrable on finite control volumes without divergent
norms or incompatible traces \cite{stratton1941}. Every conservation law in this
chapter is read as a statement about fields that can be integrated, differentiated,
and compared on finite control volumes---the same finite-energy class Stratton
\cite{stratton1941} and Harrington \cite{harrington2001} used for antennas and
waveguides.

The finite-energy requirement is more than a mathematical convenience \cite{stratton1941}. Every laboratory measurement implicitly assumes that the electromagnetic energy contained within the observation region remains finite: a field whose norm diverges on a finite control volume cannot be assigned a physically meaningful wattmeter reading, nor can it participate consistently in the flux balances developed later in this chapter \cite{poynting1884}. Dipole-class fields on a sphere $r=R$ with $k_0R=10$ satisfy $\int_{B_R}(\E^*\!\cdot\E+\H^*\!\cdot\H)\,dV<\infty$ when the Sommerfeld outgoing condition is enforced; an idealized $1/r$ template without outgoing selection is excluded because its energy integral diverges \cite{stratton1941}. Radiating phasors with declared outgoing support lie in the admissible class; non-integrable templates cannot close the conservation chain no matter how smooth their phase plots appear on a near-field display \cite{harrington2001}.

Let \(\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\) denote the analysis region,
\(\Gamma=\partial\Omega\) its boundary, and \((\E,\H)\) the phasor electric and
magnetic fields. The baseline finite-energy condition is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.energy-class}
\int_{\Omega}\!\left(\E^*\!\cdot\E+\H^*\!\cdot\H\right)\,dV<\infty,
\end{equation}
where \((\cdot)^*\) denotes complex conjugation \cite{stratton1941}. Fields that fail Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.energy-class} may still satisfy Maxwell locally in a symbolic sense, yet Poynting and stress integrals on $\partial V$ need not converge to laboratory numbers \cite{poynting1884}. Declaring integrability before transport matches what compact ranges and anechoic chambers can actually integrate \cite{hansen1988}.

Source and boundary compatibility are imposed simultaneously through
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.admissible-space}
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{ch1}}(\Omega)
=
\left\{
(\E,\H)\in L^2(\Omega)^3\times L^2(\Omega)^3:
\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma,\chi}(\E,\H)=0,\ 
\divg\Jimp-\jj\omega\rho=0
\right\},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{ch1}}(\Omega)$ is the Chapter~1 admissible field space: square-integrable Maxwell pairs on $\Omega$ whose boundary traces satisfy the material completion operator $\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma,\chi}$ and whose sources satisfy harmonic charge continuity $\divg\Jimp-\jj\omega\rho=0$ \cite{maxwell1873}. The operator $\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma,\chi}$ encodes how constitutive data $\chi$ and boundary class $\Gamma$ complete tangential traces---PEC removes tangential $\E$, PMC removes tangential $\H$, impedance sheets couple them, and exterior radiation conditions select outgoing waves \cite{stratton1941}. A field pair that solves curl equations pointwise yet violates $\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma,\chi}$ is not admissible on the declared domain even if a post-processor can fit spherical modes to its near-field samples \cite{hansen1988}. Laboratory error: prescribing $\E_t$ on an aperture without matching $\Jimp$ produces a tuple outside $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{ch1}}$; flux integrals computed from such a pair are data artifacts, not calibrated range reports \cite{stratton1941}.

\section{Maxwell Theory and Conservation Laws}
\label{sec:ch01.1}

Every angular claim inherits Maxwell's equations \cite{maxwell1873}. The Maxwell block supplies the language every later angular descriptor must speak: curl coupling ties electric and magnetic rotation; divergence ties sources to charge; constitutive completion fixes wave speed and impedance on each subdomain \cite{maxwell1873}. Uniqueness, stress, and angular balance are successive readings of the same field pair once data and boundaries are declared---not separate physics modules \cite{poynting1884}.

A finite-difference or method-of-moments solve at $1\,\mathrm{GHz}$ enforces Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-local} on its mesh \cite{stratton1941}. Angular labels assigned in post-processing---helicity sectors, modal coefficients, OAM charts---must still reduce to the same $(\E,\H)$ pair the solver enforced; modes and helicities are coordinate listings on radiative export, not independent degrees of freedom that can override curl--divergence constraints \cite{jackson1999}. When post-processing reports a ``spiral phase'' without updating $\H$ through $\curl\E=-\jj\omega\mu_0\H$, the visualization violates local compatibility even if the time snapshot looks persuasive on screen \cite{maxwell1873}.

\subsection{Maxwell equations: differential, integral, vacuum constitutive relations}
\label{sec:ch01.1.1}

Maxwell's equations admit differential, integral, and vacuum-constitutive
formulations; each answers a different class of questions in angular
electrodynamics. The \emph{differential} form governs local compatibility of
curl and divergence constraints---the point at which rotational coupling between
\(\E\) and \(\H\) is enforced. The \emph{integral} form governs finite control
surfaces and volumes---the language of flux meters, enclosed charge, and
laboratory enclosures. The \emph{vacuum constitutive relations} close the system
by linking field intensities to flux densities and propagation scales. Together
they define the Maxwell foundation from which finite-source realizability, observability, and angular structure follow \cite{stratton1941}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\VolIFigInclude{ch1-vacuum-closure-map}
\caption{Constitutive-to-propagation chain in vacuum: the same two constants \(\varepsilon_0\) and \(\mu_0\) determine flux mapping and wave kinematics.}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch1.vacuum-closure-map} closes the vacuum chain from \((\E,\H)\) to \((\mathbf{D},\mathbf{B})\) and to \(c\), \(\eta_0\), and \(k_0\) \cite{maxwell1873}.

In harmonic vacuum, a plane-wave patch $\E_z=E_0 e^{\jj k x}$ illustrates why curl coupling is not optional \cite{stratton1941}. Faraday's law gives $\H_y=(k/\omega\mu_0)E_0 e^{\jj k x}$: $\E$ and $\H$ share the same directional phase law and transverse closure. Assigning an independent azimuthal phase to $\E$ alone---a common visualization error when spiral patterns are overlaid on a single component---violates $\curl\E=-\jj\omega\mu_0\H$ and produces a field pair no laboratory probe could measure consistently on both polarization channels \cite{jackson1999}.

Harmonic fields use time factor \(\exp(-\jj\omega t)\) in a piecewise smooth region \(\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\) bounded by \(\Gamma=\partial\Omega\), with phasors \(\E(\mathbf{r})\), \(\H(\mathbf{r})\), impressed current \(\Jimp(\mathbf{r})\), charge density \(\rho(\mathbf{r})\), and vacuum parameters \(\varepsilon_0\), \(\mu_0\) in SI units \cite{stratton1941}. The differential form states local compatibility: at each point in a mesh or continuum model, how must $\E$ and $\H$ couple so that Faraday's and Amp\`ere's laws, together with Gauss's laws, close without contradiction? Finite-element and FDTD exports are trusted only when every node satisfies the same curl--divergence constraints a coaxial probe would sample on a sub-wavelength cell \cite{harrington2001}. $E_x$, $E_y$, and $E_z$ must not be treated as independent plot channels: curl coupling forbids assigning directional or angular content to a single component in isolation \cite{maxwell1873}. The differential form states how those quantities couple locally:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.maxwell-local}
\begin{aligned}
\curl \E&=-\jj\omega\mu_0\H, &
\curl \H&=\Jimp+\jj\omega\varepsilon_0\E,\\
\divg\!\left(\varepsilon_0\E\right)&=\rho, &
\divg\!\left(\mu_0\H\right)&=0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Curl coupling forbids assigning directional content to a single component in isolation \cite{stratton1941}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.maxwell-component-example}
\begin{aligned}
\partial_y E_z-\partial_z E_y&=-\jj\omega\mu_0 H_x,\\
\partial_z H_x-\partial_x H_z&=J_{\mathrm{imp},y}+\jj\omega\varepsilon_0 E_y,\\
\partial_x(\varepsilon_0 E_x)+\partial_y(\varepsilon_0 E_y)+\partial_z(\varepsilon_0 E_z)&=\rho.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-component-example} expands Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-local} in Cartesian components: each row is one curl or divergence test the solver evaluates at a mesh node \cite{stratton1941}. Subscripts $x,y,z$ denote fixed laboratory axes; $J_{\mathrm{imp},y}$ is the $y$-component of impressed current density $\Jimp$ \cite{harrington2001}. Physically, the display shows that a single-component $\E_z$ patch still couples to $\H_y$ through Faraday's law and to $\rho$ through Gauss's law---angular labels assigned to $E_z$ alone cannot be read independently of the paired $\H$ and charge fields \cite{maxwell1873}.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.maxwell-operator-block}
\mathcal{M}_\omega
\begin{bmatrix}\E\\ \H\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\curl \E+\jj\omega\mu_0\H\\
\curl \H-\Jimp-\jj\omega\varepsilon_0\E\\
\divg(\varepsilon_0\E)-\rho\\
\divg(\mu_0\H)
\end{bmatrix}
=\mathbf{0},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{M}_\omega\) is the harmonic Maxwell operator assembled from the four local laws in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-local} \cite{stratton1941}. Read $\mathcal{M}_\omega$ as a single residual vector on the field pair: the first two rows test curl coupling between $\E$ and $\H$; the third and fourth test divergence consistency with $\rho$ and magnetic flux \cite{maxwell1873}. In a finite-element export, each row is a failure channel the solver monitors---a nonzero curl residual means the $\E$--$\H$ coupling that sustains radiation is broken; a nonzero divergence residual means charge or magnetic-flux bookkeeping fails \cite{stratton1941}. Spurious $\rho$ without matching $\Jimp$ inflates these channels even when field plots appear smooth; the operator block therefore detects inconsistent source models before any angular label is assigned \cite{hansen1988}. In measurement, a mesh that passes visual inspection yet carries $\|\mathcal{M}_\omega[\E,\H]\|\neq 0$ on the feed support will produce modal coefficients that rotate correctly on paper yet disagree with watt totals on $\Gamma_R$ \cite{stratton1941}. Limitation: $\mathcal{M}_\omega$ tests local Maxwell structure only; admissible boundary traces and outgoing selection are imposed separately through $\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma,\chi}$ in Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.2} \cite{harrington2001}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.charge-continuity-harmonic}
\divg\Jimp-\jj\omega\rho=0,
\end{equation}
because \(\divg(\curl\H)=0\). Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.charge-continuity-harmonic}
is not an external add-on; it is embedded in Maxwell structure itself
\cite{stratton1941}. The same identity is recovered by differentiating
\(\divg(\varepsilon_0\E)=\rho\) in harmonic time and substituting
\(\curl\E=-\jj\omega\mu_0\H\), confirming completion consistency.

Integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.charge-continuity-harmonic} over a control volume
\(V\) with boundary \(\partial V\) and outward normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) yields
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.continuity-integral}
\int_{\partial V}\Jimp\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS
-\jj\omega\int_V\rho\,dV=0.
\end{equation}
The first term is net impressed current leaving \(V\); the second is harmonic charge variation inside \(V\), with outward unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) on \(\partial V\) \cite{maxwell1873}. Current outflow and charge accumulation must balance on every admissible control volume: a feed that injects charge without matching current through the boundary violates the same continuity law that curl--divergence structure embeds in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-local} \cite{stratton1941}.

The integral form of Maxwell equations follows from Stokes and divergence theorems.
For oriented surface \(S\subset\Omega\) with boundary \(\partial S\), oriented
volume \(V\subset\Omega\), line element \(d\boldsymbol{\ell}\), area element \(dS\),
and volume element \(dV\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.maxwell-integral}
\begin{aligned}
\oint_{\partial S}\E\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}
&=-\jj\omega\mu_0\int_S\H\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS,\\
\oint_{\partial S}\H\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}
&=\int_S\Jimp\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS
+\jj\omega\varepsilon_0\int_S\E\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS,\\
\int_{\partial V}\varepsilon_0\E\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS
&=\int_V\rho\,dV,\qquad
\int_{\partial V}\mu_0\H\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS=0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These relations are exact finite-control statements of the same laws written
locally in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-local}
\cite{stratton1941}. \phantomsection\label{prop:ch1.local-integral-equivalence}
Let \(\E,\H\in H(\curl;\Omega)\), \(\varepsilon_0\E,\mu_0\H\in H(\divg;\Omega)\),
\(\Jimp\in L^2(\Omega)^3\), and \(\rho\in L^2(\Omega)\). If \(S\) and \(V\) are
piecewise smooth oriented controls, then
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-local} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-integral} are
equivalent in weak form.

Applying Stokes and divergence theorems to the local rows gives the integral rows.
Conversely, localization of integral balances to shrinking controls recovers local
rows in the distributional sense. Regularity assumptions guarantee interchange of
limit and integration operations.

Vacuum constitutive relations supply the completion that transforms field-intensity
variables into flux-density variables:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.vacuum-constitutive}
\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon_0\E,\qquad
\mathbf{B}=\mu_0\H,\qquad
c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}}.
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathbf{D}\) is electric flux density, \(\mathbf{B}\) magnetic flux density,
and \(c\) vacuum wave speed \cite{stratton1941}. Without
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.vacuum-constitutive}, Maxwell balance laws remain under-closed in
terms of unknowns \((\E,\H,\mathbf{D},\mathbf{B})\). With completion, the model is
fully evaluable for vacuum media.

Derived vacuum scales follow directly:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.vacuum-impedance-wavenumber}
\eta_0=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}},\qquad
k_0=\omega\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}=\frac{\omega}{c},\qquad
\lambda_0=\frac{2\pi}{k_0}.
\end{equation}
\(\eta_0\) is intrinsic impedance, \(k_0\) is wavenumber, and \(\lambda_0\) is
wavelength. These scales set amplitude and phase calibration for every source-free
vacuum propagation sector \cite{stratton1941}.

Constitutive completion and wave propagation are not separate modules. The constants
that map \(\E,\H\) to \(\mathbf{D},\mathbf{B}\) also set wave speed and impedance,
so material assumptions and propagation predictions are intrinsically linked.

Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.vacuum-constitutive} into curl equations yields
second-order source-driven wave equations:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.vacuum-wave-source-driven}
\nabla^2\E+k_0^2\E=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\nabla\rho-\jj\omega\mu_0\Jimp,\qquad
\nabla^2\H+k_0^2\H=-\curl\Jimp.
\end{equation}
The \(\nabla\rho\) and \(\Jimp\) terms are electric-source forcing channels; the
\(-\curl\Jimp\) term is magnetic-source forcing for \(\H\). Equation
\eqref{eq:ch1.vacuum-wave-source-driven} clarifies that spatial curvature and
source gradients jointly shape field structure \cite{stratton1941}.

In source-free vacuum, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.vacuum-wave-source-driven} reduces to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.vacuum-helmholtz-pair}
\nabla^2\E+k_0^2\E=\mathbf{0},\qquad
\nabla^2\H+k_0^2\H=\mathbf{0},
\end{equation}
which is the vector Helmholtz pair. The reduction is valid only when both
\(\Jimp=\mathbf{0}\) and \(\rho=0\), and when the constitutive completion remains
vacuum linear isotropic.

The same source-free physics may be written in curl-curl form:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.vacuum-curl-second-order}
\curl\!\left(\mu_0^{-1}\curl \E\right)-\omega^2\varepsilon_0\E=\mathbf{0},\qquad
\curl\!\left(\varepsilon_0^{-1}\curl \H\right)-\omega^2\mu_0\H=\mathbf{0}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.vacuum-curl-second-order} and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.vacuum-helmholtz-pair} are equivalent in vacuum. Curl-curl form
is often preferable in weak formulations because it interfaces naturally with
tangential observable boundary datas and variational bilinear forms
\cite{stratton1941}.

Local plane-wave sectors in homogeneous source-free vacuum obey
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.local-plane-wave-relations}
\H=\frac{1}{\eta_0}\,\hat{\mathbf{k}}\times\E,\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\E=0,\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\H=0,
\end{equation}
where \(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\) is the unit propagation direction and \(\eta_0=\sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0}\) is vacuum intrinsic impedance \cite{stratton1941}. The three lines state transverse closure: $\H$ is perpendicular to both $\E$ and $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$, and both field vectors are transverse to propagation---the laboratory pattern of a TEM plane wave in free space \cite{jackson1999}.

Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-local}--\eqref{eq:ch1.vacuum-constitutive} describe
field evolution in the presence of \(\Jimp\) and \(\rho\), but they do not yet
specify which source distributions and boundary traces are physically realizable
on a given \(\Omega\). A mathematically writable current need not belong to the
admissible class of Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.0.3}; an interface condition written
without tangential continuity can produce apparent angular flux that is pure data
inconsistency. Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.2} couples volume
sources, interface jumps, and outer-boundary radiation data into one admissibility
chain \cite{stratton1941}.

With solenoidal \(\Jimp\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.vacuum-wave-source-driven} yields admissible fields; spurious \(\rho=\rho_0\delta(\mathbf{r})\) drives curl residuals to order unity.
Consider a filament loop of radius \(b=3\,\mathrm{cm}\) carrying phasor current
\(I_0=1\,\mathrm{A}\) at \(f=100\,\mathrm{MHz}\). Model the impressed current as
\(\Jimp=I_0\delta(\rho-b)\delta(z)\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\) in cylindrical
coordinates. Then \(\divg\Jimp=0\) because \(\partial_\phi J_\phi=0\) and the
delta support lies in a plane. With \(\rho=0\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.charge-continuity-harmonic}
is satisfied with \(\rho=0\), so the loop source is admissible in the sense of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.source-admissibility}. Substituting into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.vacuum-wave-source-driven} generates azimuthally symmetric
\(\H\) and a nonuniform \(\E\) with both radial and axial components in the near
zone. At \(kr=0.63\) the reactive fraction dominates; at \(kr=6.3\) the fields
approach the dipole radiation pattern cited in Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.2.4}.
Maxwell field specification follows from evaluating
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-operator-block} on a cylindrical grid: curl residuals
fall below \(10^{-3}\) relative to peak \(|\E|\) when charge continuity is enforced,
but rise to order unity if a spurious \(\rho=\rho_0\delta(\mathbf{r})\) is added
without adjusting \(\Jimp\) \cite{stratton1941}.

The equivalence of differential and integral forms requires
\(\E,\H\in H(\curl;\Omega)\) and flux densities in \(H(\divg;\Omega)\) as stated
in Proposition~\ref{prop:ch1.local-integral-equivalence}. Dispersive media require
frequency-dependent \(\varepsilon(\omega)\) and \(\mu(\omega)\), breaking the
algebraic completion of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.vacuum-constitutive}. Relativistic or
quantum corrections lie outside the present harmonic class. Within the vacuum harmonic class,
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-local}--\eqref{eq:ch1.local-plane-wave-relations}
form the complete local law set every later angular argument inherits
\cite{maxwell1873}.

Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.1} treats Maxwell's equations as a \emph{compatibility} system: they do not, by themselves, tell the engineer which boundary data or impressed sources belong to a realizable antenna \cite{stratton1941}. A full-wave solver enforces local curl--divergence closure at each node; a compact range reports flux through declared surfaces after outgoing selection---two readings of the same operator once data are complete \cite{harrington2001}. The vacuum constitutive chain of Figure~\ref{fig:ch1.vacuum-closure-map} is the propagation structure on which every later angular descriptor is listed, not an optional sidebar \cite{maxwell1873}. Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.2} next supplies the source, interface, and boundary tuple that converts local Maxwell laws into an identifiable boundary-value problem \cite{stratton1941}.

\subsection{Sources, boundary conditions, and physical admissibility}
\label{sec:ch01.1.2}

No radiating laboratory exists without a feed and an outer world---yet Maxwell's local equations, stated in Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.1}, do not by themselves specify which impressed currents, interface jumps, and outer-boundary radiation data belong to a physically realizable experiment \cite{stratton1941}. The boundary-value completeness question is given $(\E,\H)$ on a mesh, does a finite-source model, compatible interface data, and outgoing selection exist such that flux integrals on $\Gamma_R$ name the same physics the hardware measures? Prescribing tangential $\E_t$ on an aperture is not equivalent to specifying a source; Harrington \cite{harrington2001} and Chu \cite{chu1948} showed repeatedly that finite extent and material completion restrict what boundary templates can be synthesized. A compact-range team declares the data tuple $\mathfrak{D}$ below---impressed volume sources, surface sources, constitutive response, and boundary operator---before any angular descriptor is attached \cite{maxwell1873}.

Impressed
\emph{sources} inject current and charge; \emph{boundary conditions} fix tangential
traces and outgoing-wave selection; $\mathcal{D}_J=1$ requires the full
dataset to lie in the finite-energy class of Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.0.3}
\cite{stratton1941}. Finite antennas realize only a subset of
mathematically smooth boundary data---a theme developed in Chapter~4 \cite{stratton1941}.

Vacuum Maxwell laws from Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.1} are necessary but not
sufficient for a complete boundary-value problem. Source, interface, and outer-boundary data determine whether interior rotation exports flux or remains trapped in storage exchange with the boundary \cite{stratton1941}. Maxwell-closed requires one coupled requirement, not three independent lists.

A WR-90 feed, a dielectric window, and an open-range absorbing boundary are three different boundary stories applied to the same Maxwell operator \cite{stratton1941}. Tangential $\E$ or $\H$ continuity on metal enforces PEC or PMC traces; impedance sheets approximate thin coatings; Sommerfeld radiation selects outgoing waves. Consider a dielectric half-space with $\varepsilon_r=4$, interface at $z=0$, and a plane wave incident from $z>0$ \cite{stratton1941}. Tangential $\E$ and tangential $\H$ (apart from any impressed surface current $\mathbf{J}_s$) remain continuous across the interface: $\hat{\mathbf{z}}\times(\E_1-\E_2)=\mathbf{0}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{z}}\times(\H_1-\H_2)=\mathbf{J}_s$; with $\mathbf{J}_s=\mathbf{0}$, the reflected amplitude fixes how much power returns to the source region versus how much transmits \cite{jackson1999}. Angular transport fails silently when sources are specified on one surface and flux is reported on another without matching traces---the half-space example is the minimal warning that charge-continuous is a whole-domain statement, not a rim template \cite{harrington2001}.

Let \(\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^3\) be decomposed into piecewise smooth material
subdomains \(\{\Omega_a\}_{a=1}^{N_\Omega}\), and let
\(\Sigma_{ab}=\partial\Omega_a\cap\partial\Omega_b\) be an interface with unit
normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ab}\) directed from \(\Omega_a\) to \(\Omega_b\). Let
\(\mathbf{D}_a\) and \(\mathbf{B}_a\) denote electric and magnetic flux-density
phasors in \(\Omega_a\), \(\mathbf{J}_{s,ab}\) the electric surface-current
density on \(\Sigma_{ab}\), and \(\rho_{s,ab}\) the free surface-charge density
on \(\Sigma_{ab}\). A compact data tuple is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.data-specification-tuple}
\mathfrak{D}=
\Big(\Jimp,\rho,\{\mathbf{J}_{s,ab}\},\{\rho_{s,ab}\},\mathcal{B}_\Gamma,\mathbf{g}_\Gamma\Big),
\end{equation}
where $\mathfrak{D}$ is the complete harmonic data tuple for one solve \cite{stratton1941}. Volume impressed current $\Jimp$ and charge density $\rho$ enter through the source terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-local}; interface sheets $\mathbf{J}_{s,ab}$ and $\rho_{s,ab}$ appear when pillbox and loop limits are taken across $\Sigma_{ab}$; $\mathcal{B}_\Gamma$ is the boundary trace operator on $\Gamma=\partial\Omega$; and $\mathbf{g}_\Gamma$ is the prescribed boundary data---tangential $\E$ or $\H$ on feeds, absorbing impedances on truncations, or outgoing radiation data at infinity \cite{harrington2001}. In measurement, every compact-range file implicitly carries such a tuple even when only near-field samples are published; omitting $\Jimp$ or $\mathbf{g}_\Gamma$ leaves flux integrals undefined \cite{hansen1988}.

On \(\Gamma=\partial\Omega\), data completion is represented by a boundary
operator \(\mathcal{B}_\Gamma\) acting on the boundary traces of \((\E,\H)\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.boundary-data-operator}
\mathcal{B}_\Gamma[\E,\H]=\mathbf{g}_\Gamma,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{g}_\Gamma\) is prescribed boundary data on the declared trace class \cite{stratton1941}. Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.boundary-data-operator} closes the boundary-value problem and selects termination physics---metal, absorber, or open radiation---before uniqueness and flux reports are meaningful \cite{stratton1941}.

Interface transmission follows from the integral Maxwell balances
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-integral} by shrinking control loops and pillboxes
across \(\Sigma_{ab}\). For piecewise smooth interfaces and no impressed magnetic
sheet current, one obtains
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.interface-conditions}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ab}\times(\E_b-\E_a)&=\mathbf{0},\\
\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ab}\times(\H_b-\H_a)&=\mathbf{J}_{s,ab},\\
\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ab}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_b-\mathbf{D}_a)&=\rho_{s,ab},\\
\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ab}\cdot(\mathbf{B}_b-\mathbf{B}_a)&=0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.interface-conditions} is the transmission law on material partitions \cite{stratton1941}. Tangential $\E$ is continuous because Faraday's law integrates to zero circulation around a vanishing loop straddling $\Sigma_{ab}$; tangential $\H$ jumps by impressed surface current $\mathbf{J}_{s,ab}$; normal $\mathbf{D}$ jumps by free surface charge $\rho_{s,ab}$; and normal $\mathbf{B}$ is continuous in the absence of magnetic monopoles \cite{jackson1999}. In measurement, a thin dielectric window between a WR-90 feed and free space is modeled by these jumps; omitting $\rho_{s,ab}$ when a coated interface is present misstates reflected power \cite{collin2001}. Laboratory error: fitting only tangential $\E$ on both sides of an interface without enforcing $\mathbf{J}_{s,ab}$ produces patterns that satisfy a local plot yet violate global power balance \cite{stratton1941}.

Intermediate limiting relations leading to Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.interface-conditions}
follow from control-set shrinkage in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-integral}:
for a vanishing loop straddling \(\Sigma_{ab}\),
\[
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\oint_{\partial S_\delta}\E\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}=0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ab}\times(\E_b-\E_a)=\mathbf{0},
\]
and for a vanishing pillbox,
\[
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\partial V_\delta}\mathbf{D}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS
=\rho_{s,ab}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ab}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_b-\mathbf{D}_a)=\rho_{s,ab}.
\]
The boundary selects which tangential and normal channels transmit flux and which are screened at material partitions \cite{stratton1941}.

Metal walls, magnetic boundaries, and thin impedance coatings are the three outer termination models most antenna engineers encounter \cite{stratton1941}. A PEC wall suppresses tangential $\E$ and reflects power; a PMC wall suppresses tangential $\H$; an impedance sheet $Z_s$ couples the two traces as on a thin absorber or dielectric coating \cite{harrington2001}. The display below partitions $\Gamma$ accordingly so that each hardware termination maps to one trace class before open-region radiation is appended \cite{stratton1941}.

Outer completion is specified by partitioning
\(\Gamma=\Gamma_{\mathrm{PEC}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{PMC}}\cup\Gamma_Z\), where
\(\Gamma_{\mathrm{PEC}}\) is perfect-electric-conductor boundary,
\(\Gamma_{\mathrm{PMC}}\) is perfect-magnetic-conductor boundary, and \(\Gamma_Z\)
is an impedance boundary with surface impedance \(Z_s\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.outer-boundary-classes}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\E&=\mathbf{0}\quad &&\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{PEC}},\\
\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\H&=\mathbf{0}\quad &&\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{PMC}},\\
\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\E&=Z_s\,(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\H)\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}
\quad &&\text{on }\Gamma_Z,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) the outward unit normal on \(\Gamma\). Here
\(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\E\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\H\) are tangential
electric and magnetic traces, while \((\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\H)\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}\)
reorients tangential magnetic content into the impedance-coupled direction.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.outer-boundary-classes} therefore functions as a boundary
trace selector mathematically, and as a model of ideal reflection or finite
surface dissipation physically.

The three outer boundary classes in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.outer-boundary-classes} are limiting responses for tangential field
content. The PEC relation suppresses tangential electric trace, the PMC relation
suppresses tangential magnetic trace, and the impedance relation preserves both
while coupling them through \(Z_s\). This nesting determines which energy and
momentum channels can pass through the boundary and which are reflected
\cite{stratton1941}.

For open-region antennas and scatterers, the outer world must not send incoming waves back toward the feed \cite{stratton1941}. Compact-range absorbers and Sommerfeld radiation conditions implement the same physical requirement: transverse fields on $\Gamma$ must carry net power outward as $R\to\infty$, otherwise watt totals conflate radiative export with incoming energy that no bounded source supplied \cite{hansen1988}. The trace condition below is the asymptotic form of that outgoing selection \cite{sommerfeld1949}.

For open-region truncation, an outgoing-trace condition is often required:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.radiation-trace-condition}
\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\H
-\eta_0^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\E)\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}
=\mathbf{o}(R^{-1}),\qquad R\to\infty,
\end{equation}
where \(\eta_0=\sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0}\) and \(R\) is truncation radius.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.radiation-trace-condition} enforces asymptotically outgoing
power flow and prevents unphysical incoming-wave completion in exterior problems
\cite{stratton1941}. Laboratory error: truncating a compact range too close to the radiator without this condition selects incoming admixture; reported gain and torque then depend on absorber placement rather than on the antenna under test \cite{hansen1988}.

Every laboratory feed occupies bounded support $\Omega_s$ and must close charge with impressed $\rho$ \cite{stratton1941}. The source class below is the formal statement of that requirement: finite $L^2$ current, localized support, and harmonic continuity $\divg\Jimp-\jj\omega\rho=0$ \cite{maxwell1873}. Without it, curl-only solvers may return smooth fields that fail global charge balance on $\Omega$ \cite{harrington2001}.

Source $\mathcal{D}_J=1$ is now imposed by regularity, localization, and continuity
compatibility. Let \(\Omega_s\subseteq\Omega\) be source support. The admissible
source class is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.source-admissibility}
\Jimp\in L^2(\Omega_s)^3,\qquad
\operatorname{supp}(\Jimp)\subseteq\overline{\Omega_s},\qquad
\divg\Jimp-\jj\omega\rho=0
\ \text{in }\mathcal{D}'(\Omega).
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.source-charge-continuous} enforces finite source
energy in the local \(L^2\)-sense; the support condition enforces spatial
localization of excitation; the distributional continuity relation enforces
charge-current consistency under the adopted \(\exp(-\jj\omega t)\) phasor
convention. Taken together, these conditions exclude non-conservative source
prescriptions that would violate Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-local}. In measurement, a filament loop with $\divg\Jimp=0$ is admissible; a delta charge inserted without matching $\Jimp$ is not, even when the near-field plot appears smooth \cite{stratton1941}. Laboratory error: MoM ports that enforce $\E_t$ without charge-compatible $\Jimp$ produce coefficients that fail Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.global-charge-compatibility} at domain scale \cite{hansen1988}.

Physical $\mathcal{D}_J=1$ is not exhausted by source regularity alone. Source,
interface, and boundary channels must be jointly consistent:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.admissible-data-class}
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathbb{A}_{\omega}
\iff
\begin{cases}
\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.source-admissibility} holds}\\
\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.interface-conditions} holds on all interfaces}\\
\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.boundary-data-operator} holds with an admissible trace class}\\
\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.global-charge-compatibility} holds globally.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{A}_{\omega}$ is the frequency-dependent class of physically admissible data tuples at angular frequency $\omega$ \cite{stratton1941}. Membership in $\mathbb{A}_{\omega}$ is not a single charge-continuous test: volume sources must satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.source-admissibility}, every interface must satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.interface-conditions}, outer traces must close through Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.boundary-data-operator} on an admissible class, and global charge balance Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.global-charge-compatibility} must hold on $\Omega$ \cite{maxwell1873}. In measurement, a WR-90 feed with charge-compatible aperture current belongs to $\mathbb{A}_\omega$ above cutoff; a prescribed tangential template without $\Jimp$ extension does not, even if a finite-element solve returns smooth fields \cite{collin2001}. Laboratory error: if any channel fails, Maxwell fields may still be computed numerically but no longer inherit one coherent physical interpretation---power and torque integrals may then be computed on distinct solutions that share the same catalogue description \cite{stratton1941}.

Global charge compatibility closes the Maxwell-closed chain on whole-domain scale:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.global-charge-compatibility}
\int_{\Omega}\!\left(\divg\Jimp-\jj\omega\rho\right)\,dV
=\int_{\Gamma}\!\Jimp\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS-\jj\omega\int_{\Omega}\rho\,dV=0.
\end{equation}
Integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.charge-continuity-harmonic} over $\Omega$ and applying the divergence theorem yields Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.global-charge-compatibility} \cite{maxwell1873}. The surface integral $\int_{\Gamma}\Jimp\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS$ is net outward impressed-current flux through the domain boundary; $\jj\omega\int_{\Omega}\rho\,dV$ is the harmonic rate of charge storage inside $\Omega$ \cite{stratton1941}. In measurement, a feed that injects current without matching interior charge variation violates global balance even when local curl equations appear satisfied on the mesh \cite{harrington2001}. Laboratory error: numerical sources with $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{adm}}\gg 1$ often still produce smooth far patterns because curl residuals are small while divergence bookkeeping fails at domain scale \cite{hansen1988}.

With sources and boundaries specified, uniqueness requires one admissible dataset to define one field state. Without that answer, power and torque integrals could be computed on distinct Maxwell solutions that share the same feed description---a laboratory error familiar in resonant cavities and in truncated analysis domains placed too close to the radiator \cite{stratton1941}.

Admissibility precedes interpretation: source, interface, and boundary channels must close jointly before uniqueness or flux reports are legitimate \cite{stratton1941,maxwell1873}.

constraint Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.boundary-data-operator}. Then admissible data must
satisfy the global charge-balance identity Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.global-charge-compatibility}.

\subsection{Initial-boundary value problems and uniqueness}
\label{sec:ch01.1.3}

% CH01-NARR-EXP:01.1.3
Uniqueness on exterior domains is an outgoing-selection statement, not a numerical convenience: Sommerfeld radiation and energy bounds decide which Maxwell pair belongs to a given $\Jimp$ before flux or torque integrals are reported \cite{sommerfeld1949,stratton1941}. Two solvers that disagree in the far zone while sharing the same impressed data usually signal incomplete boundary class, not new physics \cite{harrington2001}.


Uniqueness on exterior domains is an outgoing-selection statement, not a numerical convenience \cite{sommerfeld1949}. Sommerfeld radiation and energy bounds decide which Maxwell pair belongs to a given $\Jimp$ before flux or torque integrals are reported; without that selection, two distinct $(\E,\H)$ pairs can share identical near-field samples yet differ in far-zone phase, and power or torque integrals would name different physics on the same hardware \cite{harrington2001}. Every subsequent watt, momentum, and torque integral assumes that branch has already been fixed \cite{poynting1884}.

Consider the same impressed dipole $\Jimp$ solved by two exterior codes with different absorbing boundary placements \cite{stratton1941}. Differing far-zone fields signal either incomplete data specification---wrong outgoing class, missing energy bound, or incompatible traces---or mere discretization error within one unique branch \cite{harrington2001}. When both solvers honor the same outgoing radiation condition and finite-energy class, far-zone coefficients must agree within mesh tolerance; persistent disagreement means the boundary-value problem was not fully declared, not that Maxwell admits multiple physical exports \cite{sommerfeld1949}.

Maxwell equations, sources, uniqueness, and conservation laws are the same statements FDTD and MoM codes enforce row by row \cite{maxwell1873}.

Initial-boundary specification and solution uniqueness are distinct questions
that must be joined before transport claims become identifiable. An
\emph{initial-boundary value problem} specifies both time initialization and
boundary trace class for Maxwell evolution. \emph{Uniqueness} asks whether one
admissible dataset can generate more than one electromagnetic state. Without
uniqueness, subsequent statements on power, momentum, and angular transport lose
physical identifiability \cite{stratton1941}.

Start from the time-domain formulation in a region \(\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^3\)
with boundary \(\Gamma\). Let \(\E(\mathbf{r},t)\), \(\H(\mathbf{r},t)\) be real
fields, \(\mathbf{D}\), \(\mathbf{B}\) flux densities, \(\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{imp}}\)
impressed source, and \(\rho\) free-charge density:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ibvp-time-domain}
\begin{aligned}
\curl \E(\mathbf{r},t)&=-\partial_t\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r},t),\\
\curl \H(\mathbf{r},t)&=\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{imp}}(\mathbf{r},t)+\partial_t\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{r},t),\\
\divg \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{r},t)&=\rho(\mathbf{r},t),\qquad
\divg \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r},t)=0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Initial data are \(\E(\mathbf{r},0)=\E_0(\mathbf{r})\),
\(\H(\mathbf{r},0)=\H_0(\mathbf{r})\), and boundary data are imposed through a
trace operator \(\mathcal{B}_\Gamma\). Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.ibvp-time-domain}
specifies local dynamics, but the pair \((\E_0,\H_0)\) and boundary trace class
determine which trajectory is selected from all formally admissible trajectories
\cite{stratton1941}.

For monochromatic steady operation with factor \(\exp(-\jj\omega t)\), the same
problem reduces to a frequency-domain boundary value problem:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ibvp-harmonic-bvp}
\begin{aligned}
\curl \E&=-\jj\omega\mathbf{B},\qquad
\curl \H=\Jimp+\jj\omega\mathbf{D},\\
\divg \mathbf{D}&=\rho,\qquad
\divg \mathbf{B}=0,\qquad
\mathcal{B}_\Gamma[\E,\H]=\mathbf{g}_\Gamma.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Hence, in the phasor regime, initial data enter only through consistency with the
selected harmonic branch; boundary traces and constitutive model then govern
solvability and uniqueness for each \(\omega\) \cite{stratton1941}.

To formulate uniqueness, consider two solutions
\((\E_1,\H_1)\), \((\E_2,\H_2)\) for the same dataset
\((\Jimp,\rho,\mathcal{B}_\Gamma,\mathbf{g}_\Gamma)\), and define
\(\Delta\E=\E_1-\E_2\), \(\Delta\H=\H_1-\H_2\). The difference fields satisfy the
homogeneous system
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.uniqueness-difference-system}
\begin{aligned}
\curl \Delta\E&=-\jj\omega\mu\,\Delta\H,\qquad
\curl \Delta\H=\jj\omega\varepsilon\,\Delta\E,\\
\divg(\varepsilon\Delta\E)&=0,\qquad
\divg(\mu\Delta\H)=0,\qquad
\mathcal{B}_\Gamma[\Delta\E,\Delta\H]=\mathbf{0},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
for constitutive parameters \(\varepsilon,\mu\) in the chosen model class. This
homogeneous reduction is the precise mathematical content of uniqueness testing
\cite{stratton1941}.

The uniqueness statement is therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.uniqueness-null}
\mathcal{B}_{\chi,\Gamma}[\Delta\E,\Delta\H]=0
\Longrightarrow
\Delta\E=\mathbf{0},\ \Delta\H=\mathbf{0},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{B}_{\chi,\Gamma}\) denotes the homogeneous Maxwell operator with constitutive set
\(\chi\) and boundary class \(\Gamma\) fixed \cite{stratton1941}. The left side means zero residual under homogeneous forcing; the right side means the only solution is the trivial difference field. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.uniqueness-null} is therefore a null-space condition: physically, it states that compatible data select one field state up to numerical tolerance \cite{harrington2001}. In measurement, if two solver exports differ in far-zone phase while sharing the same $\mathfrak{D}$, uniqueness has failed and flux integrals on $\Gamma_R$ are not yet identifiable \cite{sommerfeld1949}.

An energy identity for the difference state provides the key proof channel. Form
the complex Poynting vector
\(\mathbf{S}_\Delta=\tfrac12\Delta\E\times\Delta\H^{*}\), integrate over
\(\Omega\), and use the divergence theorem:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.uniqueness-energy-identity}
\int_{\Gamma}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{S}_\Delta\,dS
=-\frac12\int_{\Omega}\Delta\E\cdot\Delta\Jimp^{*}\,dV
-\jj\omega\!\int_{\Omega}\!\left(u_{\Delta H}-u_{\Delta E}\right)dV,
\end{equation}
where \(u_{\Delta E}=\tfrac14\varepsilon|\Delta\E|^{2}\),
\(u_{\Delta H}=\tfrac14\mu|\Delta\H|^{2}\), and \(\Delta\Jimp=\mathbf{0}\) for the
homogeneous problem \cite{stratton1941}. Boundary traces determine the real part of the surface term:
for passive traces it is nonnegative outgoing dissipation or radiation, while for
ideal lossless closed boundaries it vanishes \cite{stratton1941}.

A sufficient coercive estimate is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.uniqueness-estimate}
\Re\!\left\langle
\mathcal{B}_{\chi,\Gamma}[\Delta\E,\Delta\H],(\Delta\E,\Delta\H)
\right\rangle
\ge c_{\chi,\Gamma}
\left(\|\Delta\E\|_{L^2(\Omega)^3}^{2}+\|\Delta\H\|_{L^2(\Omega)^3}^{2}\right),
\end{equation}
with energy-dissipation lower bound \(c_{\chi,\Gamma}>0\), where the inner product pairs electric and magnetic residuals of the homogeneous operator \(\mathcal{B}_{\chi,\Gamma}\) \cite{stratton1941}. If the residual is zero, the left
side is zero; positivity of \(c_{\chi,\Gamma}\) forces both norms to vanish, hence
\(\Delta\E=\Delta\H=\mathbf{0}\). Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.uniqueness-estimate}
therefore links the energy identity to physical identifiability of the radiated field: coercivity is the mathematical name for the laboratory fact that passive, outgoing problems cannot sustain two distinct watt-bearing branches on the same data \cite{harrington2001}.

\phantomsection\label{prop:ch1.uniqueness-passive-closure}
Assume: (i) admissible source-boundary data from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.admissible-data-class}; (ii) passive constitutive response
\(\Re(\varepsilon)\ge 0\), \(\Re(\mu)\ge 0\) in \(\Omega\); (iii) boundary class
with nonnegative real power flux in the homogeneous difference problem. Then the
solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ibvp-harmonic-bvp} is unique.

Let \(\mathcal{F}_1=(\E_1,\H_1)\) and \(\mathcal{F}_2=(\E_2,\H_2)\) be two solutions
for the same data tuple. Set \(\Delta\E=\E_1-\E_2\), \(\Delta\H=\H_1-\H_2\). By
linearity, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.uniqueness-difference-system} holds with
\(\Delta\Jimp=\mathbf{0}\). Insert \(\mathbf{S}_\Delta=\tfrac12\Delta\E\times\Delta\H^*\)
into Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.uniqueness-energy-identity}. Passivity and the stated boundary
class force \(\Re\int_\Gamma\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{S}_\Delta\,dS\ge 0\) with
zero forcing, hence \(\Delta\E=\Delta\H=\mathbf{0}\) \cite{stratton1941}.

Uniqueness is frequency dependent because the homogeneous operator spectrum depends
on \(\omega\). Near resonant or poorly damped eigenfrequencies, null-space
amplification can occur unless radiation, loss, or additional constraints suppress
nontrivial homogeneous modes \cite{stratton1941}. A
practical indicator is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.uniqueness-condition-index}
\kappa_{\mathrm{uniq}}(\omega)=
\frac{1}{\inf_{\|(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\|=1}
\|\mathcal{B}_{\chi,\Gamma,\omega}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}]\|},
\end{equation}
where \(\kappa_{\mathrm{uniq}}(\omega)\) is the reciprocal of the smallest nontrivial residual norm of the homogeneous operator \(\mathcal{B}_{\chi,\Gamma,\omega}\) on unit field pairs \cite{stratton1941}. Large \(\kappa_{\mathrm{uniq}}(\omega)\) indicates weak coercivity and
increased sensitivity to modeling and discretization errors.

When \(\kappa_{\mathrm{uniq}}(\omega)\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.uniqueness-condition-index} is large, the homogeneous Maxwell problem admits nearly nontrivial difference modes---typical near cavity resonances, poorly damped enclosures, or truncation surfaces placed too close to the radiator \cite{stratton1941}. Two distinct field states can then match the same boundary data within numerical tolerance, so Poynting and torque integrals lose identifiability until loss, radiation, or additional constraints suppress the spurious branch \cite{harrington2001}.

Uniqueness is not asserted directly from Maxwell equations alone,
but from Maxwell equations plus admissible constitutive and boundary completion. The
difference-system route is therefore central rather than optional.

Within the admitted class, however, the initial-boundary formulation and
uniqueness criterion guarantee that one admissible dataset corresponds to one
physically interpretable electromagnetic state. Strong gain, ill-conditioned
discretizations, or non-selfadjoint inverse completions can still produce multiple
spurious field branches; those cases require augmented analysis beyond passive coercivity
\cite{stratton1941}.

Once uniqueness fixes the field branch, Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.4} integrates the same Maxwell pair for energy and linear momentum, and Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.5} closes the angular balance. Conservation laws are not postulates added to Maxwell theory; they are consequences of the field equations on the unique solution selected by admissible data \cite{poynting1884}. Every watt, newton, and newton-metre report in the remainder of Chapter~\ref{ch:01} is an integral over a field pair already selected by $\mathfrak{D}$, $\mathcal{B}_\Gamma$, and outgoing radiation data---not an independent measurement module that could contradict Maxwell if a modal fit looks persuasive \cite{stratton1941}.

\subsection{Energy, linear momentum, and Maxwell stress tensor}
\label{sec:ch01.1.4}

A wattmeter on $\partial V$ reports cycle-averaged power crossing a declared surface; a radiation-pressure experiment reports directed momentum received; the Maxwell stress tensor $\mathbf{T}$ unifies those surface questions. Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.3} fixed which field branch is being integrated; here the same branch is metered for energy and linear momentum before angular transport is discussed in Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.5} \cite{poynting1884}.

The stress tensor links field algebra and transducer readings: traction $\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ is what a thin absorber samples, while $\divg\mathbf{T}$ balances impressed force density in the volume \cite{poynting1884}. Radiation pressure on a flat plate and Poynting flux through a sphere are two surface readings of the same Maxwell pair once the branch is unique \cite{maxwell1873}.

On a normally incident plane wave striking a perfect absorber, one measures a time-averaged Poynting flux $\langle S_z\rangle$ through the front face and a radiation pressure $p=\langle t_z\rangle$ on the same surface \cite{poynting1884}. The classical relation $p=\langle S_z\rangle/c$ in vacuum: energy flux and directed momentum flux are two readings of the Maxwell stress tensor $\mathbf{T}$, not independent postulates \cite{stratton1941}. Laboratory error: reporting pressure from $|\E|^2$ alone without the $\H$ coupling that enters $\mathbf{T}$ misstates momentum transfer even when the wattmeter reading is correct \cite{maxwell1873}.

Uniqueness makes flux budgets identifiable: a single field branch now supports
measurable surface integrals. Under the harmonic convention $\exp(-\jj\omega t)$, define
cycle-averaged Poynting vector
\(\mathbf{S}=\tfrac{1}{2}\E\times\H^*\), where \((\cdot)^*\) is complex
conjugation \cite{stratton1941}. Define stored-energy densities
\(u_E=\tfrac{1}{4}\varepsilon_0\E\cdot\E^*\) and
\(u_H=\tfrac{1}{4}\mu_0\H\cdot\H^*\). The complex power balance is
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.poynting-complex}
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.poynting-average}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.dual-invariant-poynting}
\divg\mathbf{S}
=-\frac{1}{2}\E\cdot\Jimp^*
-\jj\omega\left(u_H-u_E\right).
\end{equation}
Physically, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.dual-invariant-poynting} states that net power leaving a differential volume equals impressed work on $\Jimp$ plus reactive exchange between electric and magnetic storage \cite{poynting1884}. The divergence term gives net power outflow
density from a differential control volume. The source term
\(\E\cdot\Jimp^*/2\) is electromagnetic work rate on impressed current. The term
\(-\jj\omega(u_H-u_E)\) is reactive exchange between magnetic and electric storage
channels. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.poynting-complex} therefore couples transport,
source work, and storage imbalance in one local conservation statement \cite{stratton1941}.

Poynting's original energy-flux vector \cite{poynting1884} predates the complex
phasor form but already encoded the central idea: electromagnetic energy moves
through space in a direction determined by the cross product of field intensities.
The cycle-averaged generalization \(\mathbf{S}=\tfrac12\E\times\H^*\) used here is
the harmonic specialization standard in time-harmonic field theory
\cite{stratton1941}. When the same fields interact with matter,
momentum bookkeeping acquires additional subtlety: Minkowski's symmetric
energy-momentum tensor for material media \cite{minkowski1908} and Abraham's
alternative stress form disagree on how much momentum is stored in a dielectric,
yet both reduce to the vacuum Maxwell stress in source-free exterior regions
where this chapter's angular-transport criteria are evaluated
\cite{stratton1941}. This chapter develops transport on
vacuum-side control surfaces first; material-specific momentum splits enter through
Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.5.1} only where detectors and absorbers require them.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.poynting-real-imag-split}
\begin{aligned}
\divg\Re(\mathbf{S})&=-\frac12\Re\!\left(\E\cdot\Jimp^*\right),\\
\divg\Im(\mathbf{S})&=-\frac12\Im\!\left(\E\cdot\Jimp^*\right)-\omega(u_H-u_E).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.poynting-real-imag-split} separates transported average
power from reactive storage exchange under one harmonic field state.

\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.dual-invariant-energy}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.power-balance-integral}
\int_{\partial V}\mathbf{S}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS
=-\frac12\int_V\E\cdot\Jimp^*\,dV
-\jj\omega\int_V(u_H-u_E)\,dV.
\end{equation}
Here $V\subset\Omega$ is a control volume with outward normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ on $\partial V$ \cite{stratton1941}. The surface integral is net complex power through $\partial V$; the volume terms are impressed work and reactive storage imbalance inside $V$ \cite{poynting1884}. This control-volume relation provides direct finite-set interpretation for probe
and aperture measurements \cite{hansen1988}.

Splitting into real and imaginary parts gives the measurement-relevant separation:
\(\Re(\mathbf{S})\) controls net transported power density and
\(\Im(\mathbf{S})\) controls reactive circulation associated with local
near-field energy exchange. This distinction prevents reactive phase structure from
being misread as long-range momentum transport.

Linear momentum flux is represented through the time-averaged Maxwell stress
tensor. In vacuum its dyadic form is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.maxwell-stress}
\mathbf{T}
=\frac{1}{2}\Re\!\left[
\varepsilon_0\,\E\E^*
+\mu_0\,\H\H^*
-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}
\left(\varepsilon_0\,\E\cdot\E^*+\mu_0\,\H\cdot\H^*\right)
\right],
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{I}\) is the identity dyad and juxtaposition such as \(\E\E^*\)
is dyadic product. Operand by operand, the first two dyads are directional field
intensity contributions, while the isotropic subtraction term
\(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}(\cdots)\) removes trace pressure so that directional
tractions are represented correctly. The real-part operator keeps physically
observable time-averaged transfer.

As a complete object, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-stress} relates electromagnetic
field state to mechanical traction on material control surfaces. For unit normal
\(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\), the traction density is
\(\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\), and the net force on closed
surface \(\partial V\) is \(\mathbf{F}=\int_{\partial V}\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS\).
No new numbered equation is introduced for these definitions because they are
direct functional uses of the already-established stress tensor.

Read $\mathbf{T}$ as the mechanical face of the same Maxwell pair already used for Poynting flux: traction $\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ is what a thin absorbing sheet or torsion fixture samples at the surface, while $\divg\mathbf{T}$ balances impressed force density in the volume. Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.5} rotates this same traction picture into angular flux through $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{T}$ \cite{poynting1884}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.linear-momentum-balance}
\divg\mathbf{T}+\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{em}}=\mathbf{0},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{em}}\) denotes the time-averaged force density
delivered by the field to matter. This equation is the local momentum-balance
counterpart of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.poynting-complex}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.energy-momentum-directional-ratio}
\Pi_{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}
:=
\frac{\hat{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\Re(\mathbf{S})}
 {c\,\hat{\mathbf{q}}\cdot(\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{q}})+\epsilon_\Pi},
\end{equation}
for unit probe direction \(\hat{\mathbf{q}}\), vacuum speed \(c\), and fixed
nondimensional regularizer \(\epsilon_\Pi>0\) (not vacuum permittivity) \cite{stratton1941}. \(\Pi_{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}\) quantifies
directional agreement between energetic and mechanical transport channels: unity indicates that Poynting flux and normal stress traction align along the probe direction on the same Maxwell branch \cite{poynting1884}.

\subsection{Angular momentum conservation and torque balance}
\label{sec:ch01.1.5}

Angular momentum conservation ties rotating feeds and torque measurements to the same flux statements Poynting analyzed \cite{poynting1884}. If Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.4} answered how power and linear momentum cross a surface, the display below answers the rotational analogue: can the same qualified field export angular action that a torsion balance or flux integrator can reconcile with mechanical torque on matter? The answer is not read from phase colour plots; it is read from $\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}$, $\mathbf{M}$, and the torque channel tied to Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-balance-integral} \cite{beth1936}.

Beth's torsion experiment established the laboratory pattern: predict torque from $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{T}$, measure mechanical response on matter, and treat discrepancy as channel or calibration error \cite{beth1936}. Angular-momentum density $\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}$ and flux tensor $\mathbf{M}$ rotate the linear-momentum budget imported from Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.4}; they do not introduce independent postulates \cite{poynting1884}.

Circular polarization on a half-wave plate is Beth's archetype: mechanical torque requires net outward angular-flux through the enclosing surface, while polarization conversion redistributes angular action between field and matter rather than creating new Maxwell content \cite{beth1936}. On the range, attributing torque to phase colour alone fails when $\oint \mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS$ on a radiative shell is below the transducer noise floor \cite{stratton1941}.

Angular-momentum conservation and torque balance are coupled requirements.
Conservation means that rotational field storage and rotational flux obey a local
balance law derived from Maxwell dynamics. Torque balance means that any net
change in field angular momentum is accounted by mechanical torque delivered to
matter. Without this pair, visual phase winding cannot be interpreted as physical
rotational transport \cite{poynting1884}.

Let \(\mathbf{r}\) be position vector measured from a declared origin
\(\mathbf{r}_0\), let \(\mathbf{T}\) be Maxwell stress tensor from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-stress}, and define angular-flux tensor
\(\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{T}\). The local rotational balance law is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.angular-momentum-balance}
\frac{\partial\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}}{\partial t}
+\divg\mathbf{M}
=-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{mech}},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\) is electromagnetic angular-momentum density and
\(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{mech}}\) is mechanical torque density on matter.
\(\partial_t\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\) tracks local rotational storage variation,
\(\divg\mathbf{M}\) tracks net angular-flux leakage, and the right-hand side is
field-to-matter exchange channel \cite{stratton1941}.

Integrated control-volume form over \(V\subset\Omega\) with boundary
\(\partial V\) and outward normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.rotation-am-invariance}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.angular-balance-integral}
\frac{d}{dt}\int_V \mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\,dV
+\int_{\partial V}\mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS
=-\int_V \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{mech}}\,dV.
\end{equation}
The first term is stored angular content inside \(V\); the second is exported or
imported angular flux through \(\partial V\); the right term is net mechanical
torque uptake by enclosed matter.

In linear isotropic vacuum, a useful density split is
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.rotation-generator-split}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.angular-density-split}
\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}
=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{em}}+\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{em}},\qquad
\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{em}}=\varepsilon_0\mu_0\,\Re(\mathbf{S}),
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{em}}\) is linear-momentum density and
\(\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{em}}\) is spin-associated local contribution. The first term
is orbital-type contribution about \(\mathbf{r}_0\), while \(\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{em}}\)
captures intrinsic polarization-linked contribution under the adopted averaging
convention \cite{stratton1941}.

Belinfante's 1940 field-theoretic analysis \cite{belinfante1940} clarified why such
splits are inevitable rather than optional. Canonical momentum density and kinetic
(symmetric) momentum density differ by the divergence of a spin current; angular
momentum density correspondingly decomposes into an orbital part
\(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{kin}}\) plus an intrinsic spin part. The
split in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-density-split} is one Maxwellian realization of
that structure for electromagnetic fields, not a separate postulate. Beth's 1936
measurement of torque on a wave plate under circular polarization
\cite{beth1936} provided early experimental support that the spin channel carries
mechanical consequences, while later orbital-indexed beam experiments
\cite{allen1992} extended the vocabulary to spatial phase gradients. Chapter~\ref{ch:01}
unifies these lineages under one conservation law: whichever historical label is
used, completion must follow Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-balance-integral}.

For torque observables about axis \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\), define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.torque-observable}
\tau_{\hat{\mathbf{u}}}(V)
=-\hat{\mathbf{u}}\cdot
\left(
\frac{d}{dt}\int_V \mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\,dV
+\int_{\partial V}\mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS
\right),
\end{equation}
which is the axis-projected version of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-balance-integral}.
This form makes measurement geometry explicit: torque is a projected transport
functional, not a scalar label inferred from phase pictures.

Directional rotational-transport concentration can be quantified by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.angular-flux-directional-index}
\xi_J(\theta,\phi;r)
:=
\frac{\left\|\mathbf{M}(r,\theta,\phi)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}\right\|}
 {\int_{4\pi}\left\|\mathbf{M}(r,\theta',\phi')\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}'\right\|\,d\Omega'+\epsilon_J},
\end{equation}
with \(\epsilon_J>0\) a fixed nondimensional regularizer (not vacuum permittivity) \cite{stratton1941}. The index \(\xi_J(\theta,\phi;r)\) names which
solid-angle channels dominate exported angular flux at radius $r$ and therefore dominate
direction-selective torque response on a declared axis \cite{beth1936}. In measurement, a main-lobe peak in $\xi_J$ signals where $\mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ concentrates; it does not by itself establish net torque until Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.torque-observable} is evaluated on the same control surface \cite{harrington2001}.

Reproducible torque comparison across experiments requires consistent declaration of control-surface orientation, measurement-axis unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{u}}$, and origin $\mathbf{r}_0$ entering $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{T}$ \cite{stratton1941}. Changing any of these geometric bookkeepers rotates flux projections without altering the underlying Maxwell pair; cross-laboratory reports must therefore publish $(\mathbf{r}_0,\hat{\mathbf{u}},\partial V)$ alongside torque curves \cite{beth1936}.
Consider two admissible states \(\mathcal{F}_1,\mathcal{F}_2\) at common radius
\(r\), with equal total radiated power but different directional angular-flux
densities \(\mathbf{M}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}\neq
\mathbf{M}_2\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}\) on a nonzero-measure subset of \(4\pi\). Then
there exists at least one physically admissible interaction kernel for which the
measured torque differs between the two states.

Use Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.torque-observable} with a directional weighting kernel that
selects the subset where fluxes differ. Equal total power constrains only energetic
integral transport and does not constrain the angular-flux distribution channel by
channel; hence projected torque functionals can differ \cite{stratton1941}.

This criterion provides the direct connection to Section~\ref{sec:ch01.2}: directional
and topological descriptors introduced there are physically
credible only if they satisfy \(\mathcal{Q}_{J}=1\), so interpretation remains
anchored to transport and torque observables rather than visual morphology alone.

Transport gives consequence to structure once Maxwell, Maxwell-closed, and uniqueness close on the declared domain \cite{poynting1884}. Section~\ref{sec:ch01.2} therefore separates three layers analysis must not collapse: appearance in phase and polarization patterns, realizability on finite sources, and transport through qualified flux integrals \cite{belinfante1940}. Covariance, gauge, and measurement refinements in Sections~\ref{sec:ch01.3}--\ref{sec:ch01.5} adjust how those layers are reported; they do not replace the transport tests fixed here \cite{stratton1941}.

\section{Angular Structure of Electromagnetic Fields}
\label{sec:ch01.2}

Maxwell theory and conservation laws in Section~\ref{sec:ch01.1} fix what finite sources may produce. Section~\ref{sec:ch01.2} asks a narrower question: how directional and rotational organization appears in $(\E,\H)$ itself, and which readouts survive gauge ambiguity and finite measurement \cite{belinfante1940}. Angular structure is field physics---organized phase, polarization, and flux in the Maxwell pair---not a post-processing label pasted onto a numerical output.

Qualified Maxwell solutions can look directionally organized long before they export
angular action. Section~\ref{sec:ch01.2} separates directional, phase, polarization,
and Poynting topology from the narrower class that \emph{transports} qualified flux
\cite{belinfante1940}. Phase winding and modal index remain representation-facing until flux integrals and measurement readouts establish export.

Organized phase on a near-field probe pattern is not yet angular transport \cite{allen1992}. Directional dependence, helical topology, and Poynting circulation each test a different projection of $(\E,\H)$; only the combination that survives flux integrals on a declared control surface qualifies as exported action \cite{belinfante1940}. A Laguerre--Gaussian-like waist scan at $z=0$ may show a vivid azimuthal phase ramp, yet the measurement that confirms transport on $\Gamma_R$ is outward $\oint \mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS$ on a radiative shell enclosing the source; reactive circulation on a sub-wavelength ring can satisfy visualization criteria while failing $\mathcal{C}_3$ \cite{beth1936}. The subsections below move from geometry of direction to topology of phase to the three-part transport test $\mathcal{C}_1$, $\mathcal{C}_2$, $\mathcal{C}_3$ \cite{belinfante1940}.

A realizable pattern may still fail transport, and a transporting pattern may still
lie below instrument resolution. The degrees-of-freedom question from
Section~\ref{sec:ch01.0} therefore becomes directional channel count on observation
spheres and the first operational split between field existence and data recoverability
\cite{hansen1988}.

Every major result in this section answers one question: \emph{what is angular
electromagnetic structure?} The answer is directional organization of
\((\E,\H)\) capable of transporting measurable rotational action through qualified
flux integrals and torque channels---not a false-colour phase pattern, not an orbital
index pasted onto a beam, and not a modal name substituted for Maxwellian transport
\cite{maxwell1873}.
\subsection{Rotating source versus rotating pattern}
\label{sec:ch01.2.0}

When a spiral isophase pattern appears on an outgoing sphere $S_R$, does that pattern necessarily imply exported angular momentum, and does it distinguish a mechanically rotating feed from a stationary phased array? The two configurations may look identical on a phase camera; flux and torque channels need not agree \cite{beth1936}. Chapter~\ref{ch:01} must not equate azimuthal phase winding with transport: Allen \emph{et al.} \cite{allen1992} showed that beams can carry a countable azimuthal index, yet counter-rotating superpositions with equal power cancel net export while preserving vivid phase structure---the counterexample developed fully in Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.6.2}. The qualified flux--torque pair must be recorded, not the spiral portrait alone \cite{stratton1941}.

The signature comparison below is the chapter's first concrete warning against equating spiral phase with exported angular momentum. A mechanically rotating feed imposes motion on $\Omega_s$ and generally sustains a torque channel on the mount; a fixed feed with azimuthal phase may reproduce the same spiral isophases on $\Gamma_R$ yet cancel torque on a torsion fixture \cite{beth1936}. Keep those two laboratory configurations separate before any modal index is assigned \cite{harrington2001}.

Two laboratory configurations can produce indistinguishable azimuthal phase patterns on an
outgoing sphere yet differ in mechanical torque and in qualified angular-momentum
export. The first rotates the source---currents, polarization basis, or feed

synthesis. On a distant sphere both may exhibit the same spiral isophase contours;
only flux integrals and torque channels decide whether rotational action is
transported \cite{beth1936}.

\subsection{Directional dependence and angular momentum}
\label{sec:ch01.2.1}

% CH01-NARR-EXP:01.2.1
The flux pair $(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J)$ ties Poynting transport to angular-momentum action on declared control surfaces \cite{poynting1884,belinfante1940,beth1936}. Beth torque and spherical harmonic labels are downstream readouts of that pair---not independent postulates that can override Maxwell closure \cite{stratton1941}.


The flux pair $(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J)$ ties Poynting transport to angular-momentum action on declared control surfaces \cite{poynting1884}. Beth torque and spherical harmonic labels are downstream readouts of that pair---not independent postulates that can override Maxwell field specification \cite{stratton1941}.

Directional dependence is the first filter between ``a field exists'' and ``a field exports action in a chosen sector.'' A pattern may radiate power isotropically yet carry no net directional angular flux; conversely, a highly structured near field may circulate energy locally without exporting the channel a far-zone instrument resolves \cite{stratton1941}. Allen \emph{et al.} \cite{allen1992} showed that Laguerre--Gaussian modes carry a well-defined azimuthal index and that mode converters transfer that index reversibly; those results belong to the physics of structured light, yet Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ch1.directional-power-density} and~\eqref{eq:ch1.directional-am-flux} remain the measurement-facing quantities that decide export on a chosen sphere \cite{stratton1941}. Directional radiation patterns were analyzed in antenna theory long before orbital angular momentum became a standalone keyword \cite{stratton1941}.

For an $\ell=1$ dipole on $\Gamma_R$, direction-resolved angular-flux density may integrate to zero over $\phi$ while total radiated power remains non-zero \cite{stratton1941}. Symmetric dipole patterns cancel orbital flux channels even when $|m=1|$ sectors carry power in a harmonic listing---a common source of confusion when modal coefficients are mistaken for torque reports \cite{hansen1988}. A field
distribution can satisfy global energy conservation and still fail to produce
directional rotational transfer in the channels used by a detector or scatterer.
Therefore, angular interpretation proceeds through direction-resolved
transport observables rather than through visual phase morphology
\cite{stratton1941}.

The transition from Section~\ref{sec:ch01.1} is governed by the strict qualification
\(\mathcal{Q}_{J}=1\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.torque-transport-qualified-gate}. Any directional angular
claim made below is physically accepted only when that criterion remains satisfied; if
the criterion fails, the same directional pattern is treated as kinematic appearance
without qualified rotational transfer.

Let \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}=\hat{\mathbf{r}}(\theta,\phi)\) be radial unit vector on a
radius-\(r\) sphere and \(d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\) the solid-angle
measure. Using \(\mathbf{S}\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.poynting-complex}, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.directional-power-density}
\Pi(\theta,\phi;r)=
r^2\,\Re\!\left\{\mathbf{S}(r,\theta,\phi)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here $\Pi(\theta,\phi;r)$ is directional power flux per unit solid angle at radius $r$: watts per steradian in the outward channel $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ \cite{poynting1884}. The product $\mathbf{S}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ selects energetic transport along that ray; the factor $r^2$ converts flux density to a solid-angle channel measure on a sphere of radius $r$ \cite{stratton1941}. In measurement, a calibrated range integrates $\Pi$ over probe directions to build a radiation pattern; that pattern is energetic transport, not yet rotational action \cite{hansen1988}.

The integrated total is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.directional-power-integral}
P_{\mathrm{rad}}(r)=\int_{4\pi}\Pi(\theta,\phi;r)\,d\Omega.
\end{equation}
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.worked-rad-power}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.directional-power-integral} is a projection of channelwise
structure. Equal projected totals do not imply equal directional physics.

For rotational transport, define from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-momentum-balance}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.directional-am-flux}
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J(\theta,\phi;r)=
r^2\,\mathbf{M}(r,\theta,\phi)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}.
\end{equation}
Here $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J$ is the directional angular-momentum flux density: the solid-angle channel through which stress-flux $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{T}$ exports rotational action along $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ \cite{beth1936}. The pair $(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J)$ is the minimal dataset required to separate energetic transport from rotational transport channel by channel \cite{stratton1941}. Laboratory error: reporting $\Pi$ alone and inferring torque from main-lobe direction mistakes power beaming for qualified angular export \cite{harrington2001}.

The corresponding total outward angular flux is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.directional-am-total}
\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{rad}}(r)=
\int_{4\pi}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J(\theta,\phi;r)\,d\Omega.
\end{equation}
This vector integral cannot replace channelwise structure because cancellation among
opposing angular channels can leave a small total despite large directional flux
magnitudes.

A normalized directional efficiency observable is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.directional-transport-ratio}
\boldsymbol{\eta}_J(\theta,\phi;r)=
\frac{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J(\theta,\phi;r)}
{\Pi(\theta,\phi;r)+\epsilon_\Pi},
\qquad \epsilon_\Pi>0.
\end{equation}
\(\epsilon_\Pi\) regularizes channels near energetic nulls. Large
\(\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_J\|\) identifies directions where modest power supports strong
rotational transfer, which is common in highly structured emission fields.

In antenna practice, directional diagrams display \(\Pi(\theta,\phi)\) long before
angular-flux distributions are computed; the analysis makes the second map explicit
so that a main-lobe power peak is never automatically read as a torque-capable
channel. Stratton's systematic field-theory treatment \cite{stratton1941} already
 emphasized that multipole expansions organize angular dependence of radiation,
while Belinfante's decomposition \cite{belinfante1940} warns that local spin and
orbital labels are not unique even when totals are fixed. Both lessons are absorbed
here by keeping \(\Pi\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J\) distinct until their ratio
passes the qualification criteria below \cite{stratton1941}.

Channel qualification is imposed explicitly by
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.topology-qualified-gate}
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.gauge-qualified-decision}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.directional-qualified-gate}
\mathcal{Q}_{J,\Omega}(\theta,\phi;r)=1
\iff
\Big(
\Pi(\theta,\phi;r)>0,\
\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J(\theta,\phi;r)\|>0,\
\mathcal{Q}_{J}=1
\Big).
\end{equation}
Thus only channels with \(\mathcal{Q}_{J,\Omega}=1\) contribute to transport-
qualified angular interpretation in later subsections.

Directional centroids are defined by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.directional-power-centroid}
\mathbf{c}_P(r)=
\frac{\int_{4\pi}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\,\Pi(\theta,\phi;r)\,d\Omega}
{\int_{4\pi}\Pi(\theta,\phi;r)\,d\Omega},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.directional-am-centroid}
\mathbf{c}_J(r)=
\frac{\int_{4\pi}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\,
\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J(\theta,\phi;r)\|\,d\Omega}
{\int_{4\pi}\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J(\theta,\phi;r)\|\,d\Omega}.
\end{equation}
Mismatch \(\mathbf{c}_P\neq\mathbf{c}_J\) means dominant energetic direction and
dominant rotational direction are geometrically displaced, a measurable effect in
torque-sensitive coupling experiments.

\phantomsection\label{prop:ch1.directional-nonequivalence}
Let two radiative states \(\mathcal{F}_1,\mathcal{F}_2\) at radius \(r\) satisfy
\(P_{\mathrm{rad}}^{(1)}(r)=P_{\mathrm{rad}}^{(2)}(r)\). If there exists a set
\(\mathcal{U}\subset4\pi\) of nonzero measure where
\(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J^{(1)}\neq\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J^{(2)}\) and
\(\mathcal{Q}_{J,\Omega}=1\), then the states are rotationally non-equivalent for
direction-selective interactions.

The equality of \(P_{\mathrm{rad}}\) constrains only
\(\int_{4\pi}\Pi\,d\Omega\). It does not constrain channelwise rotational flux.
Define admissible interaction kernel \(K(\theta,\phi)\) supported on \(\mathcal{U}\):
\[
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{obs}}=
\int_{4\pi}K(\theta,\phi)\,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J(\theta,\phi;r)\,d\Omega.
\]
Since qualified channels differ, observed torque differs.

Integrated totals are terminal reductions of richer channel fields. Different channel topology
can produce nearly identical totals while yielding different torque outcomes.

Origin declaration remains essential because angular transport is moment-based.
Changing origin re-weights channel moments even when global balances remain
unchanged. For reproducible comparison, all directional flux and torque statements
must use the same origin convention fixed in Section~\ref{sec:ch01.1.5}.

Directional observables represent physical transport
only when outward channels are well-defined and \(\mathcal{Q}_{J}=1\) remains valid.
In strongly reactive near zones, strongly nonlocal media, or rapidly modulated
boundaries, extra decomposition terms may be necessary before \(\Pi\) and
\(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J\) are treated as direct rotational-transfer observables
\cite{stratton1941}.

For fully polarized harmonic fields, the Jones vector
\(\mathbf{j}_p=(E_\theta,E_\phi)^\mathsf{T}\) evolves under ideal polarizers and
wave plates by unitary \(2\times 2\) matrices. A rotation of the polarization
state through angle \(2\psi\) about axis \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_p\) on the Poincar\'e
sphere corresponds to
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.polarization-ellipse-angles}
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.rodrigues-rotation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.jones-rotation}
\mathbf{j}_p'
=
\exp\!\left(-\jj\,\psi\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}_p\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)\mathbf{j}_p,
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z)\) are Pauli matrices and
\(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_p\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) is the generator of polarization
rotations \cite{stratton1941}. Mathematically,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.jones-rotation} is a unitary map on \(\mathbb{C}^2\); physically,
it describes how a lossless optical element reorients the oscillation ellipse without
changing \(S_0\) for a single coherent mode. Beth's quartz-plate measurement
\cite{beth1936} is the mechanical readout of such a rotation when circularly
polarized light transfers angular momentum to the plate.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.coherency-matrix}
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\langle E_x E_x^*\rangle & \langle E_x E_y^*\rangle \\
\langle E_y E_x^*\rangle & \langle E_y E_y^*\rangle
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where angle brackets denote narrowband time averaging on a declared transverse plane.
For a source, $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$ summarizes partial polarization of transverse components;
for radiation, it fixes the Stokes vector seen by a polarimetric receiver; for measurement,
only rotation-invariant traces of $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$ are directly comparable across rotated frames \cite{stratton1941}.
Partial polarization requires the coherency matrix of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.coherency-matrix}. The degree of polarization
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.polarization-degree}
p=\frac{\sqrt{S_1^2+S_2^2+S_3^2}}{S_0+\epsilon_p},
\qquad 0\le p\le 1,
\end{equation}
with \(\epsilon_p>0\), measures how much of the field energy participates in a
polarized component versus an unpolarized background. When \(p<1\), Stokes parameters
describe an ensemble average and \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_P=\curl\mathbf{P}\) can remain
large locally while exported torque averages to zero because incoherent components
do not carry a definite handedness. Narrowband laboratory sources often have
\(p>0.95\); thermal or multiply-scattered fields may have \(p\ll 1\), which must be
reported before interpreting \(S_3\) as spin angular momentum
\cite{stratton1941}.

Directional dependence is therefore a \emph{channel} question: which angular-momentum sheet on $S^{2}$ carries flux, and does that sheet survive the qualification gate $\mathcal{Q}_J=1$ before any Beth or OAM label is attached \cite{belinfante1940}? A Stokes polarimeter on $\Gamma_R$ records $S_0,S_1,S_2,S_3$---a local field statistic---not yet a transport verdict; polarization degree $p$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.polarization-degree} must be reported before $S_3$ is read as spin content \cite{stratton1941}. Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.2.2} separates helical phase topology from export, using paraxial vortex templates only as naming devices once flux integrals are declared \cite{allen1992}.

\phantomsection\label{sec:ch01.2.2}
Without plane-wave templates, a helical phase ramp on a transverse slice appears to establish orbital angular momentum by geometry alone \cite{allen1992}. The decisive quantity is not the winding of $\arg E$ on a plane but the signed channel imbalance that survives integration of $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J$ on a control surface enclosing the source \cite{belinfante1940}. Allen \emph{et al.} \cite{allen1992} gave paraxial optics a compact modal vocabulary; Stratton \cite{stratton1941} and Beth \cite{beth1936} already taught that flux and torque integrals, not phase colour, close the measurement sequence. \emph{Topology} must be distinguished from \emph{transport}: between \emph{topology}---where phase winds and Poynting circulation may be large---and \emph{transport}---where net export requires a non-canceling angular-flux channel \cite{poynting1884}. Helical phase on a paraxial slice is therefore a naming device for azimuthal winding; export still requires channel-resolved flux on a declared surface \cite{allen1992}. A paraxial beam with $m=+1$ at the waist, followed by a $\lambda/4$ plate that converts to linear polarization, changes $\oint \mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS$ on $\Gamma_R$---not merely the phase colour of the near-field display \cite{allen1992}. Polarization conversion redistributes spin and orbital contributions between field and matter; the flux integral on a declared radiative shell is the consistency check that separates transport from appearance \cite{beth1936}.

Allen \emph{et al.} \cite{allen1992} identified paraxial modes
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.phase-winding-number}
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.phase-singularity-condition}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.lg-vortex-field}
E_\phi(\rho,\phi,z)
=
E_0
\left(\frac{\rho}{w_0}\right)^{|m|}
L_p^{|m|}\!\left(\frac{2\rho^2}{w_0^2}\right)
e^{-\rho^2/w_0^2}\,
e^{\jj(m\phi+k_0 z)},
\end{equation}
where \(L_p^{|m|}\) is an associated Laguerre polynomial, \(w_0\) is beam waist,
and integer \(m\) is azimuthal index. For \(p=0\), \(|m|=1\), the intensity vanishes
on the axis (\(\rho=0\)) while the phase winds by \(2\pi\) on a circuit about the
origin: Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.phase-winding-number} gives \(q=m\). The Poynting vector
circulates azimuthally on the intensity ring, so \(\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_P\|\) is
large there even when the beam is collimated. Export of structured angular flux
requires a single signed channel: superposing Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.lg-vortex-field}
with its conjugate winding produces the same standing pattern as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-counter-rotating-superposition} in
Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.6.2}. Topology and polarization therefore remain
measurement stages; transport is fixed only after channel imbalance is measured on a
control surface \cite{allen1992}.

On a transverse plane, critical points of \(\mathbf{P}=\Re\{\mathbf{S}\}\) satisfy
\(\partial_x P_x+\partial_y P_y=0\) with Jacobian
\(\mathcal{J}_P=\partial(P_x,P_y)/\partial(x,y)\). Vortex-type regions have
\(\operatorname{tr}\mathcal{J}_P\approx 0\) and \(\det\mathcal{J}_P>0\); saddle-type
regions have \(\det\mathcal{J}_P<0\). Define the flow-structure index
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.poynting-flow-index}
\mathcal{F}_P=
\frac{\int_A \|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_P\|\,dA}
{\left|\int_A \divg\mathbf{P}\,dA\right|+\epsilon_F},
\end{equation}
where \(A\) is a declared patch and \(\epsilon_F>0\). Large \(\mathcal{F}_P\) with
small net \(\int_A\divg\mathbf{P}\,dA\) signals predominantly circulating storage;
large net divergence with moderate \(\mathcal{F}_P\) signals export-dominated
patches. Poynting's original flow-line pictures \cite{poynting1884} emphasized this
distinction long before Stokes vectors were standard: a beautiful vortex pattern in
\(\mathbf{P}\) is not, by itself, a radiative torque channel. Poynting flow-line diagrams predated Stokes polarimetry and OAM beam nomenclature \cite{poynting1884}; the same test still applies: circulation in $\mathbf{P}$ diagnoses storage or interference texture, while $\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS$ on a radiative shell diagnoses export \cite{beth1936}. Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.2.3} states the positive transport criteria $\mathcal{C}_1$, $\mathcal{C}_2$, $\mathcal{C}_3$ that convert these qualitative warnings into admissibility tests used throughout the remainder of Chapter~\ref{ch:01} \cite{stratton1941}.

\subsection{Conditions for angular-momentum transport}
\label{sec:ch01.2.3}

Subsections~\ref{sec:ch01.2.1} and~\ref{sec:ch01.2.2} established that rich phase and polarization patterns need not export angular action. Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.2.3} closes the positive side of that distinction:

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\caption{Schematic of the three-condition admissibility pathway for angular-momentum transport in Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.2.3}.}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch1.transport-triad-map} routes the transport triad through \(\mathcal{C}_1\), \(\mathcal{C}_2\), and \(\mathcal{C}_3\) before the qualified completion \(\mathcal{D}_J=1\) \cite{poynting1884}.

A vortex-core photograph can circulate the eye while the far-zone flux integral
vanishes---display without transport. The operational question posed here is when
angular organization exports qualified flux rather than merely decorating the field:
Section~\ref{sec:ch01.1.5} tied angular momentum to stress and torque;

\cite{stratton1941}.

From a Maxwellian standpoint, transport is not a visual property of phase fronts;
it is a balance property. A pattern that satisfies the wave equation and even
radiates power can still fail angular transport if directional angular-flux channels
cancel, if conservation completion is violated on the chosen control volume, or if
outward power support is absent in the sector where angular structure appears strongest.
The three-part test developed below encodes exactly these three laboratory errors as positive acceptance criteria $\mathcal{C}_1,\mathcal{C}_2,\mathcal{C}_3$ \cite{stratton1941}. Transport qualification is operational: $\mathcal{C}_1$ demands non-canceling directional angular-flux channels; $\mathcal{C}_2$ closes conservation on the control volume; $\mathcal{C}_3$ requires outward power support in the sector where angular structure appears \cite{belinfante1940}. Modern beam laboratories and Beth-style torque experiments differ in hardware yet report the same triad---flux balance, conservation, and radiative support \cite{hansen1988}.

Counter-propagating Laguerre--Gaussian modes of equal amplitude furnish a sobering triad test \cite{belinfante1940}. Rich phase structure may satisfy visualization criteria while channel cancellation fails $\mathcal{C}_1$ even when $\mathcal{C}_2$ holds: existence of organized phase is not equivalent to qualified transport through the three-part criteria developed in this section \cite{allen1992}. Once this qualification is
fixed, it carries unchanged through Sections~\ref{sec:ch01.3}--\ref{sec:ch01.6}:
covariance, gauge, and measurement refinements adjust reports, but they cannot resurrect
transport where the three-part test already failed at the flux level.

Historically, experiments established pieces of this three-part test before it was
written as one unified criterion. Beth \cite{beth1936} showed that polarization-coupled angular
momentum produces measurable torque; Allen \emph{et al.} \cite{allen1992} showed
that beam modes can carry a countable azimuthal index. Chapter~\ref{ch:01} requires
that any modern claim satisfy \(\mathcal{C}_1,\mathcal{C}_2,\mathcal{C}_3\)
simultaneously so that experimental, modal, and field-theoretic languages describe
the same qualified state \cite{belinfante1940}.

A field configuration exhibits genuine angular-momentum transport only when
directional structure, conservation completion, and outward energetic support hold
simultaneously. Any one in isolation is insufficient. The diagnostic question is
not whether angular patterns are visible, but whether they survive flux accounting,
torque completion, and $\mathcal{D}_J=1$ filtering under the transport-qualified regime
\cite{stratton1941}.

Let \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J(\theta,\phi;r)\) be the directional angular-momentum flux
map from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.directional-am-flux}, and let
\(\Pi(\theta,\phi;r)\) be directional power flux from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.directional-power-density}. A necessary directional condition
for nontrivial net transport over a chosen observation sphere is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.am-net-transport-condition}
\int_{4\pi}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J(\theta,\phi;r)\,d\Omega \neq \mathbf{0}.
\end{equation}
That is, local angular
structure must accumulate into a nonzero exported balance; otherwise the pattern
is confined to internal recirculation or compensating directional cancellation.

Directional sufficiency, however, cannot be judged from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.am-net-transport-condition} alone. The same field must satisfy
global conservation consistency with the integral law already established in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-balance-integral}. That relation rules out interpretive
shortcuts: exported angular transport must appear in torque or storage channels,
not in phase appearance alone \cite{stratton1941}.

Transport distinction also requires separation of outward propagative support
from reactive exchange. Using \(\mathbf{P}=\Re\{\mathbf{S}\}\) from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.poynting-average}, outward channel support for a directional
sector \(\Delta\Omega\subseteq 4\pi\) is checked by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.propagative-support}
\int_{\Delta\Omega}\Pi(\theta,\phi;r)\,d\Omega>0.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.propagative-support} enforces that angular-momentum
claims are tied to sectors carrying net outward power; in sectors where this
condition fails, strong local field complexity may persist without transport
credibility.

The strict carryover from Section~\ref{sec:ch01.1} remains active:
\(\mathcal{Q}_{J}=1\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.torque-transport-qualified-gate}
is mandatory before any directional claim is accepted as physical transport.

The three relations above define a three-part transport test:
\[
\mathcal{C}_1:\ \text{directional angular export }
(\eqref{eq:ch1.am-net-transport-condition}),\qquad
\mathcal{C}_2:\ \text{control-balance consistency }
(\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-balance-integral}),\qquad
\mathcal{C}_3:\ \text{propagative support }
(\eqref{eq:ch1.propagative-support}).
\]
Taken together, $\mathcal{C}_1$ asks whether angular flux integrates to a nonzero export, $\mathcal{C}_2$ asks whether that export fits the same conservation completion as torque and storage on the chosen volume, and $\mathcal{C}_3$ asks whether the claimed sector actually carries outward power. A pattern that fails any one test may still look impressive in a post-processed visualization; it should not be promoted to a transport claim in a laboratory report \cite{stratton1941}.

To convert this three-part test into a quantitative Maxwell-closed test, introduce the
dimensionless transport index
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.transport-charge-continuous-index}
\Lambda_J(r)=
\frac{\left\|\int_{4\pi}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J(\theta,\phi;r)\,d\Omega\right\|}
{\int_{4\pi}\Pi(\theta,\phi;r)\,d\Omega+\epsilon_\Lambda},
\qquad \epsilon_\Lambda>0,
\end{equation}
where \(\|\cdot\|\) is Euclidean norm and \(\epsilon_\Lambda\) regularizes weak
radiation windows. The numerator is net exported angular transport; the
denominator is total exported power. Large \(\Lambda_J\) indicates high rotational
transfer efficiency per unit outward power. Interpreting \(\Lambda_J\) requires the
same caution applied to any ratio of flux quantities: a small denominator from weak
radiation can inflate the index even when absolute transport is negligible. That is
why Table~\ref{tab:ch1.triad-numeric-dipole} reports \(P_{\mathrm{rad}}\) and
\(\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{rad}}\|\) alongside \(\Lambda_J\), and why
\(\mathcal{C}_1\) is never evaluated from \(\Lambda_J\) alone
\cite{stratton1941}.

Directional cancellation can be measured independently through
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.directional-cancellation-factor}
\mathcal{K}_J(r)=
\frac{
\left\|\int_{4\pi}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J\,d\Omega\right\|
}{
\int_{4\pi}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J\right\|\,d\Omega+\epsilon_K},
\qquad 0\le \mathcal{K}_J\le 1,\ \epsilon_K>0.
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathcal{K}_J\approx 1\) means directional channels add coherently toward
net export, while \(\mathcal{K}_J\approx 0\) means local angular flux is largely
self-canceling over solid angle. This factor prevents a common interpretive
failure where large local \(\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J\|\) is mistaken for strong net
transport despite angular cancellation.

Because Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-balance-integral} couples storage, flux, and mechanical
torque, the same criterion can be written in torque-normalized form:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.torque-consistency-residual}
\mathcal{R}_\tau(V)=
\left\|
\frac{d}{dt}\int_V \mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\,dV
+\int_{\partial V}\mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS
+\int_V \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{mech}}\,dV
\right\|,
\end{equation}
with \(\mathcal{R}_\tau(V)=0\) for exact conservation completion on admissible data.
Finite \(\mathcal{R}_\tau\) in experiments quantifies mismatch from measurement
noise, truncation, or model incompleteness.

For operational comparison, define
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.local-density-qualified-decision}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.transport-qualified-decision}
\mathcal{D}_J(r)=1
\iff
\Big(
\mathcal{Q}_J=1,\
\Lambda_J(r)\ge \Lambda_{\min},\
\mathcal{K}_J(r)\ge K_{\min},\
\mathcal{R}_\tau(V)\le R_{\max}
\Big),
\end{equation}
where \(\Lambda_{\min}>0\), \(0<K_{\min}\le 1\), and \(R_{\max}\ge 0\) are declared
study thresholds. This converts three-part logic into a reproducible decision rule for
analysis and measurement procedures.

\phantomsection\label{prop:ch1.transport-triad-sufficiency}
Assume a harmonic field state in the admissible class of Section~\ref{sec:ch01.1},
and suppose \(\mathcal{C}_1,\mathcal{C}_2,\mathcal{C}_3\) hold on an observation
sphere of radius \(r\). Then angular structure descriptors in that state admit a
transport interpretation: exported angular-momentum flux is nontrivial, consistent
with control-volume conservation, and supported by outward energy propagation.

\(\mathcal{C}_1\) gives nonzero net angular export; \(\mathcal{C}_3\) ties export to
outward power support; \(\mathcal{C}_2\) closes the balance of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-balance-integral}. Together they exclude
representation-only phase patterns \cite{stratton1941}.

Thus the proposition establishes structural sufficiency, while
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.transport-qualified-decision} establishes operational acceptance.

Transport qualification is
a conjunction, not a menu. A failure in any branch demotes the state from
transport-capable to representation-rich but transport-incomplete. This
distinction is operationally useful in both analysis and measurement, where
complex phase and polarization fields may satisfy one or two branches but not all
three.

Origin declaration remains part of the condition set because angular moments are
computed through \(\mathbf{r}\times(\cdot)\). Changing origin can redistribute
directional partitions and alter modal bookkeeping; transport conclusions are
therefore comparable only when origin and boundary geometry are fixed across the
comparison set.

Practically, the decision sequence is: compute \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_J\) and
\(\Pi\), evaluate \(\Lambda_J\) and \(\mathcal{K}_J\), and confirm
\(\mathcal{R}_\tau\) within admissible tolerance, enforce \(\mathcal{Q}_J=1\), and
apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.transport-qualified-decision}. This ordering removes false
positives where vivid local structure is present but exported rotational action is
weak, self-canceling, or torque-inconsistent.

The three-part test and derived indices above assume harmonic
steady state, sufficiently resolved directional sampling on \(4\pi\), and
constitutive completion in the linear local regime used in this chapter. Strongly
time-varying boundaries, broadband pulse windows, and highly nonlocal response can
require generalized transport operators before the same thresholds are interpreted
without ambiguity \cite{stratton1941}.

\subsubsection*{Active and passive rotations; transformation of vectors, tensors, and dyads}

A torsion balance and a spherical near-field range may report the same Maxwell pair in different component bases \cite{beth1936}. The covariance material below fixes the transformation laws that keep those reports objective: vectors, tensors, and dyads rotate through distinct but linked rules, and traction $\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ must transform as a vector when the laboratory frame rotates \cite{stratton1941}. Mishandling dyadic rotation is a frequent source of spurious torque when origins or bases shift between numerical export and fixture calibration \cite{harrington2001}.

Rotate a laboratory frame and the components of \(\E\) reorder; rotate the stress
dyad incorrectly and a torsion balance reports torque that Maxwell never supplied.
Vectors, tensors, and dyads each obey a distinct transformation law under proper
rotations \(R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\); the Maxwell stress tensor is the rank-two object
whose mishandling is most costly in angular work \cite{stratton1941}. A torque report
computed in a rotated frame must transform $\mathbf{T}$ as a dyad via
$\mathbf{T}'=R\mathbf{T}R^\top$, while traction $\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}$
transforms as a vector; covariance failures at any stage propagate into false
conclusions about stress transfer and torque prediction \cite{beth1936}.

For proper rotation \(R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\), with \(R^\top R=I\), \(\det R=1\), let
\(\mathbf{v}=v_j\hat{\mathbf{e}}_j\) be vector in orthonormal basis
\(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_j\). A laboratory vector---Poynting flux, electric field, or traction---is a single geometric object whose components reorder under rotation but whose magnitude and direction relations are fixed by Maxwell physics \cite{stratton1941}. Component transformation is
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.active-rotation-action}
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.infinitesimal-active-rotation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.vector-rotation-components}
v_i' = R_{ij}v_j.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.vector-rotation-components} is the component-level
realization of active/passive vector rotation from Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.3.1}.
After a turntable roll, a spherical near-field range reports precisely this relabeling: the same $|E|$ on $S_R$ expressed in rotated $\hat{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ components \cite{hansen1988}. Rotating the \emph{source} must be distinguished from rotating the \emph{component chart}: rotating the \emph{source} (Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.0.2}) and passively rotating the \emph{component chart} while holding the feed fixed \cite{stratton1941}.

For second-rank tensor \(\mathbf{T}\) with components \(T_{pq}\),
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.vector-observable-covariance}
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.passive-rotation-action}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.tensor-rotation-rule}
\mathbf{T}' = R\,\mathbf{T}\,R^\top,
\qquad
T'_{ij}=R_{ip}R_{jq}T_{pq}.
\end{equation}
The two factors \(R_{ip}\), \(R_{jq}\) transform each tensor slot. This is the
objectivity law ensuring that stress tensor
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-stress} rotates geometrically rather than numerically.
Mishandling this law---treating $T_{ij}$ as scalars that rotate independently---is a frequent source of spurious torque when numerical exports are compared with torsion-fixture data \cite{beth1936}. Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.tensor-rotation-rule} states that Maxwell stress is a geometric object acting on surface normals, not a table of numbers indexed by coordinate axes \cite{stratton1941}.

Active and passive rotation conventions are paired by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.active-passive-duality}
(R v)_i=R_{ij}v_j,\qquad (R^{-1}v)_i=R_{ji}v_j,
\end{equation}
so that fixed physics in a rotated chart is relabeled by \(R^{-1}\) when components are
passively transformed \cite{stratton1941}.

Tensor invariants satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.tensor-invariant-trace}
\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{T}')=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{T}),\qquad
\|\mathbf{T}'\|_F=\|\mathbf{T}\|_F,
\end{equation}
because \(\operatorname{tr}(R\mathbf{T}R^\top)=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{T})\) and
orthogonality of \(R\) preserves Frobenius inner product.

If \(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{q}_k=\lambda_k\mathbf{q}_k\), then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.stress-eigen-covariance}
\mathbf{T}'(R\mathbf{q}_k)=\lambda_k(R\mathbf{q}_k),
\end{equation}
so eigenvalues are objective and principal directions rotate covariantly.

For dyadic map \(\mathbf{D}\) defined by \(\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{u}\):
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.rotation-group-action}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.dyadic-covariance}
\mathbf{D}' = R\,\mathbf{D}\,R^\top,
\qquad
\mathbf{w}'=\mathbf{D}'\cdot\mathbf{u}'=R\mathbf{w},
\end{equation}
with \(\mathbf{u}'=R\mathbf{u}\). This equation establishes slotwise equivariance:
rotating input and operator yields rotated output.

Rank-one dyad \(\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^\top\) transforms as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.rank1-dyad-rotation}
\mathbf{D}'=R\mathbf{a}\,(R\mathbf{b})^\top.
\end{equation}
Both generating directions rotate, preserving dyadic rank and directional coupling
interpretation used in mode-projection constructions.

Composition compatibility follows:
\[
\mathbf{D}'' = R_2(R_1\mathbf{D}R_1^\top)R_2^\top
=(R_2R_1)\mathbf{D}(R_2R_1)^\top.
\]
Hence staged rotations and single composite rotation are algebraically identical.

Transformation rules organize into
one operational flow from algebraic object to measurable traction output.

With \(\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) and
\(\hat{\mathbf{n}}'=R\hat{\mathbf{n}}\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.traction-rotation-covariance}
\mathbf{t}'=\mathbf{T}'\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}' = R\mathbf{t}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.traction-rotation-covariance} is the direct connection from
covariance algebra to measurable mechanical response.

The Maxwell stress tensor of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-stress} is symmetric in
vacuum regions, \(\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}^\top\), and Maxwell \cite{maxwell1873}
introduced the associated mechanical force on conductors precisely through traction
\(\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\). Under \(R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)\), symmetry is
preserved because
\(\mathbf{T}'=(R\mathbf{T}R^\top)^\top=R\mathbf{T}^\top R^\top=\mathbf{T}'\).
The eigenvalue problem therefore commutes with rotation:
if \(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{q}_k=\lambda_k\mathbf{q}_k\), then
\(\mathbf{T}'(R\mathbf{q}_k)=\lambda_k(R\mathbf{q}_k)\), extending
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-eigen-covariance} to the full spectral decomposition.
Principal stresses \(\lambda_k\) are scalars reportable without frame tag; principal
directions \(R\mathbf{q}_k\) rotate covariantly and must be compared only after
declaring active or passive convention from Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.3.1}.

Stratton \cite{stratton1941} emphasized that dyadic Green functions
\(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}')\) for the vector Helmholtz
operator transform as
\(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}'(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}')=
R\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}(R^{-1}\mathbf{r},R^{-1}\mathbf{r}')\,R^\top\)
under simultaneous rotation of observation and source coordinates. Slotwise
equivariance in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.dyadic-covariance} is the local differential
realization of this global operator law. For a rank-one modal coupling
\(\mathbf{D}_{mn}=\mathbf{f}_m\mathbf{g}_n^\top\) between modes \(m\) and \(n\),
rotation re-expresses both factor vectors, but the scalar coupling strength
\(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{D}_{mn}^\top\mathbf{D}_{mn})\) remains invariant when
\(\mathbf{f}_m,\mathbf{g}_n\) are co-rotated.

The mathematical content of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ch1.vector-rotation-components}--\eqref{eq:ch1.dyadic-covariance} is that Maxwell physics is rotation-covariant when sources, media, and boundaries share the same symmetry class declared in Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.0.2} \cite{stratton1941}. Rotating the \emph{chart} must be distinguished from rotating the \emph{feed}: passive component relabeling leaves the impressed current fixed, whereas active rotation of $\Jimp$ changes the export unless $\kappa_{\mathrm{rot}}$ vanishes \cite{hansen1988}. Numerical exports must publish which convention was used for $\mathbf{T}'=R\mathbf{T}R^\top$ and for traction $\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}$, because a torque channel computed in a rotated frame is not comparable to a torsion wire calibrated in the laboratory basis without explicit covariance \cite{beth1936}. Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.3.3} next fixes origin dependence---a separate bookkeeping requirement from rotation, but equally decisive for torque reports \cite{stratton1941}.

\phantomsection\label{sec:ch01.3.3}
\subsubsection*{Origin dependence and translational covariance}

Torque reports computed at different reference points on the same fixture must not be identified \cite{beth1936}. Maxwell stress traction $\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ is objective under rigid rotation, but angular momentum flux $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{T}$ depends on the origin $\mathbf{r}_0$ from which $\mathbf{r}$ is measured---a fact already visible in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-balance-integral} and made operational for calibrated channels in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-origin-shift-torque} of Section~\ref{sec:ch01.5} \cite{stratton1941}. Translational covariance does not mean that torque is origin-independent; it means that two laboratories comparing $y_\tau$ must translate reports through the shift law $-\Delta\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{det}}$ before debating polarization content \cite{harrington2001}. A torsion balance records mechanical reaction about a declared suspension axis; changing $\mathbf{r}_0$ without updating that declaration injects spurious $z$-torque proportional to net force when $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_\tau=\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ \cite{beth1936}. The mathematical statements in Section~\ref{sec:ch01.3} for rotation of $\mathbf{T}$ therefore pair with an equally strict bookkeeping rule for origins: frame-independent observables are scalars such as $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{T})$ and principal-stress eigenvalues; vector torque functionals are frame-tagged unless origin and axis are published with the data \cite{stratton1941}.

\phantomsection\label{sec:ch01.3.4}
\subsubsection*{Invariants and frame-independent observables}

Only certain contractions survive arbitrary proper rotations without additional convention tags \cite{stratton1941}. Equations~\eqref{eq:ch1.tensor-invariant-trace} and~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-eigen-covariance} state that traces, Frobenius norms, and eigenvalues of the Maxwell stress tensor are objective scalars, while principal directions rotate covariantly with the laboratory frame \cite{maxwell1873}. The important distinction is not between ``tensor components'' and ``physics,'' but between \emph{invariant reports}---watts, principal stresses, coupling strengths $\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{D}_{mn}^\top\mathbf{D}_{mn})$---and \emph{covariant reports}---traction, torque, and directional flux densities---that require explicit active/passive rotation convention from Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.3.1} \cite{stratton1941}. Traction $\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ transforms as a vector under Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.traction-rotation-covariance}, which is why a pressure pad and a torsion wire read objective mechanical load once the surface orientation is fixed, whereas a tabulation of $T_{ij}$ components without rotation law is not yet a laboratory report \cite{beth1936}. Section~\ref{sec:ch01.4} next asks which combinations of $(\E,\H)$ remain meaningful when potentials are relabeled---a separate invariance question from rotation, but equally decisive for angular-momentum discourse \cite{belinfante1940}.

Section~\ref{sec:ch01.3} fixes one discipline for the display below: \emph{invariant scalars}---traces, eigenvalues, coupling strengths---may be compared across laboratories without convention tags, while \emph{covariant vectors and torque functionals} require published rotation law, origin, and axis before any angular-momentum headline is written \cite{stratton1941}. Gauge relabeling in Section~\ref{sec:ch01.4} tests a different layer of invariance: potentials may change while flux integrals on $\partial V$ remain fixed only when the observables are constructed from $(\E,\H)$ rather than from $\mathbf{A}$ or $\phi$ alone \cite{belinfante1940}.

This definition is inherited by every later chapter: realizability limits what finite
sources can generate; observability limits what instruments can report; transport
qualification limits which directional patterns carry measurable rotational action
\cite{maxwell1873}. Angular structure is not a phase picture,
not a vortex label, and not a modal index standing alone---it is Maxwellian field

Potentials are coordinate labels on a fixed Maxwell branch: under $\mathbf{A}'=\mathbf{A}+\nabla\chi$ and $\phi'=\phi-\jj\omega\chi$, the physical pair \((\E,\H)\) is unchanged while $\mathbf{A}$ and $\phi$ need not agree pointwise \cite{maxwell1873}. Near-field post-processors often export $\mathbf{A}$; wattmeters and torsion balances respond to $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}$. Any comparison between the two must fix gauge and outgoing branch before potential-based plots are treated as laboratory readings \cite{harrington2001}.

At $f=1\,\mathrm{GHz}$, Coulomb and Lorenz potential pairs may reproduce identical tangential $\E_t$ on $r=R$ while differing at an interior sample point \cite{stratton1941}. Only $\E$, $\H$, and gauge-invariant curls are physical; scalar potentials differ by a relabeling that does not alter wattmeter or torque integrals when declared consistently \cite{jackson1999}.

\subsection{Gauge-invariant observables, helicity, and dual symmetry}
\label{sec:ch01.4.3}

Gauge freedom is not a defect of Maxwell theory; it is a reminder that local potentials are not laboratory observables.

Helicity and dual-symmetric combinations survive gauge relabeling because they are built from \((\E,\H)\) invariants on $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rad}}$, not from $\mathbf{A}$ or $\Phi$ alone \cite{belinfante1940}. Two laboratories comparing ``helicity flip'' on a range must therefore specify the same flux gauge and the same radiative branch before interpreting sign changes \cite{hansen1988}.

Adding $\nabla\chi$ to $\mathbf{A}$ with $\Phi=-\jj\omega\chi$ leaves outward $\oint \mathbf{S}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS$ and gauge-invariant helicity flux unchanged, while local densities such as $\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}$ may shift by a pure gauge term \cite{belinfante1940}. Reports invariant under gauge relabeling must be distinguished from diagnostic plots that are convention-dependent \cite{stratton1941}. The quantities that survive potential relabeling and rigid-frame motion while retaining transport meaning are helicity, dual-symmetric combinations, and flux-normalized reports built from \((\E,\H)\)---precisely the objects a calibrated range can compare across laboratories \cite{belinfante1940}. In the radiative regime, helicity quantifies the signed overlap
between electric and magnetic rotational content; dual symmetry links field patterns
under the duality rotation that preserves free-space Maxwell structure
\cite{stratton1941}. The admissibility specification below promotes
only those combinations that close gauge variation, respect covariance, and connect
to qualified flux channels.

Let the phasor field state be \(\mathcal{F}=(\E,\H)\) in a linear isotropic region
with scalar permittivity \(\varepsilon\), scalar permeability \(\mu\), angular
frequency \(\omega\), and intrinsic impedance
\(\eta=\sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}\). Let \((\mathbf{A},\phi)\) denote a potential pair
related to the physical fields by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.potential-field-relations}
\E=-\nabla\phi+\jj\omega\mathbf{A},\qquad \mathbf{B}=\curl\mathbf{A}=\mu\H,
\end{equation}
Gauge relabeling of potentials is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.gauge-transform}
\mathbf{A}'=\mathbf{A}+\nabla\chi,\qquad \phi'=\phi-\jj\omega\chi,\qquad \E'=\E,\ \H'=\H.
\end{equation}
so that \(\E\) and \(\mathbf{B}=\mu\H\) are reproduced through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.potential-field-relations}. A scalar report \(\mathcal{O}_s\),
vector report \(\mathbf{O}_v\), and pseudoscalar report \(\mathcal{O}_p\) are
accepted as gauge-invariant observables only if all three components of the
$\mathcal{D}_J=1$ specification are satisfied:
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.gauge-observable-invariance}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.observable-admissibility-triad}
\mathcal{O}_\alpha[\mathbf{A}',\phi']=\mathcal{O}_\alpha[\mathbf{A},\phi],\qquad
\mathcal{O}_\alpha[\mathcal{A}_R\mathcal{F}]=\mathcal{R}_\alpha(R)\,\mathcal{O}_\alpha[\mathcal{F}],
\qquad
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{loc}}=1,
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha\in\{s,v,p\}\), \((\mathbf{A}',\phi')\) is generated by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.gauge-transform}, \(\mathcal{A}_R\) is active spatial rotation
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.active-rotation-action}, and \(\mathcal{R}_\alpha(R)\) is
representation operator: identity for scalars, \(R\) for vectors, and parity-aware sign
rule for pseudoscalars. The final clause imports the local-density criterion of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.local-density-qualified-decision}; therefore a report can be
algebraically elegant yet rejected if local gauge contamination survives.

Maps such as $\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{loc}}=\mathbf{r}\times(\varepsilon_0\E)$ inherit the gauge freedom of the potential used to construct $\E$, whereas traction and angular-flux integrals built from $\mathbf{T}$ and $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{T}$ remain stable on declared surfaces when \((\E,\H)\) is fixed \cite{belinfante1940}. A vivid near-field vortex plot therefore shows a convention-tagged coordinate on conserved transport, not an independent observable \cite{maxwell1873}.

Within a control volume $V_g$ of radius $R=2\lambda$ about a dipole, Coulomb and Lorenz constructions of $\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{loc}}$ need not agree pointwise \cite{belinfante1940}. With consistent reporting, $\oint_{\partial V_g}\mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS$ is stable under $\mathbf{A}\mapsto\mathbf{A}+\nabla\chi$, whereas pointwise $\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{loc}}$ shifts unless gauge is fixed---the content of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.local-density-qualified-decision} \cite{stratton1941}.

A gauge-invariant observable here means more than formal cancellation in a symbolic
expression: the reported quantity must be insensitive to potential-gauge choice and
also carry a consistent rigid-frame transformation law, so that two observers using
rotated coordinates assign equivalent physical content to the same field
configuration
\cite{stratton1941}. 

Within the admissible class, helicity is introduced as a field-intrinsic
pseudoscalar density, following the handedness measure standard in classical
field theory \cite{stratton1941}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.helicity-density}
h(\mathbf{r})=
\frac{1}{2\omega}\Im\!\left\{
\varepsilon\,\E^*\cdot(\curl\E)
\;+\;
\mu\,\H^*\cdot(\curl\H)
\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here \((\cdot)^*\) denotes complex conjugation, \(\Im\{\cdot\}\) extracts imaginary
part, and \(\curl=\nabla\times\). The electric contribution
\(\varepsilon\,\E^*\cdot(\curl\E)\) measures local rotational phase-structure of
\(\E\), while the magnetic contribution
\(\mu\,\H^*\cdot(\curl\H)\) measures the corresponding structure in \(\H\). Division
by \(2\omega\) places helicity on a frequency-normalized scale so that comparisons
between configurations at fixed \(\omega\) are not dominated by trivial frequency
rescaling.

Gauge robustness follows directly because \(h\) is built from \(\E\), \(\H\), and
their curls, each invariant under potential gauge relabeling for fixed physical
fields. At the operator level, under
\(\mathbf{A}'=\mathbf{A}+\nabla\chi\), \(\E'=\E\) because
\(-\nabla\phi'+\jj\omega\mathbf{A}'=
-\nabla\phi+\jj\omega\mathbf{A}+\nabla(\jj\omega\chi-\jj\omega\chi)\).
Similarly \(\curl\E\) is unchanged because \(\curl\nabla\chi=\mathbf{0}\). The
magnetic sector follows from \(\H=\mathbf{B}/\mu\) and \(\mathbf{B}=\curl\mathbf{A}\).
Stratton \cite{stratton1941} listed \(\E\), \(\H\), \(\mathbf{B}\), and quadratic
energy expressions among the primary field observables precisely because they factor
through gauge-invariant curls.

The dual rotation on field pairs is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.duality-rotation}
\E'=\cos\xi\,\E+\sin\xi\,\eta\H,\qquad
\H'=-\sin\xi\,\E/\eta+\cos\xi\,\H,\qquad \xi\in\mathbb{R}.
\end{equation}
The dual rotation Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.duality-rotation} is not a gauge symmetry: it
mixes \(\E\) and \(\H\) while leaving the source-free Maxwell system invariant in
isotropic media. Infinitesimal dual variation \(\xi=\delta\xi\) gives
\(\delta\E=\delta\xi\,\eta\H\) and \(\delta\H=-\delta\xi\,\E/\eta\) to first order.
Helicity density responds as a pseudoscalar should: for circular polarization states
specified through Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.1}, \(h\) changes sign under
\(\xi=\pi/2\) because electric and magnetic roles interchange with a handedness
flip. Dual defect index \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{dual}}\) from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.duality-defect-index} quantifies failure of this symmetry when
\(\varepsilon,\mu\) vary spatially or when conduction loss breaks phase coherence
between \(\E\) and \(\H\).

Integrated helicity over volume \(V\) connects to flux through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.helicity-balance}. In harmonic steady state,
\(\partial_t h=0\) and source-free regions give
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.helicity-flux-integral}
\int_{\partial V}\mathbf{f}_h\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS
=
\int_V q_h\,dV,
\end{equation}
so net helicity export is controlled by material conversion \(q_h\). For vacuum
\(V\) enclosing a radiative source, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.helicity-flux-integral} plays
the same limiting role as Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-balance-integral}: only flux
through \(\partial V\) is externally reportable.

The local gauge-variation identity is
\phantomsection\label{eq:ch1.gauge-field-invariance-check}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.helicity-density-wave-reduction}
\delta_\chi h
\equiv
h[\mathbf{A}',\phi']-h[\mathbf{A},\phi]
=
0.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.helicity-density-wave-reduction} is the local statement that
helicity density is gauge-objective when evaluated from physical field data.

Helicity reports are accepted only when gauge variation, dual symmetry, and local-density
qualification all pass:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.helicity-observable-decision}
\mathcal{D}_{h}=1
\iff
\Big(
\delta_\chi h=0,\
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{dual}}=1,\
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{loc}}=1
\Big).
\end{equation}

Noether-type continuity structure associated with dual rotations gives the local
helicity-balance law
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.helicity-balance}
\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}
+\divg\mathbf{f}_h
=
-q_h,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{f}_h\) is helicity flux density and \(q_h\) is material helicity
conversion rate. In source-free homogeneous domains \(q_h=0\), so helicity is
transported but not created or annihilated locally
\cite{stratton1941}. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.helicity-balance} is the
helicity counterpart of the momentum and angular-momentum conservation forms
developed earlier in Sections~\ref{sec:ch01.1}--\ref{sec:ch01.2}.

With gauge-invariant observable criteria, helicity completion, and dual-symmetry
validity now fixed, the next block defines operational observables in measurement
language without conflating representation algebra with measurable transport channels
\cite{maxwell1873}.

After gauge and frame invariance are fixed, the experimenter's question becomes:
by what measurement sequence does a symmetry-admissible field quantity become a
reported number with declared uncertainty and reproducibility? An operational
definition is a closed map from field state to instrument response, from response
to estimator, and from estimator to a traceable calibrated reporting convention
\cite{poynting1884}.

Maxwell \cite{maxwell1873} insisted that measurable quantities must be defined through
operations on bodies---forces, couples, and energy transfers---rather than through
auxiliary potentials alone. The operational measurement specification below makes that
Maxwellian criterion explicit for angular diagnostics \cite{stratton1941}.

Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be a field state that already passes
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.observable-charge-continuous-triad}. Let
\(\mathcal{M}_\alpha\) denote the forward measurement operator for channel setting
\(\alpha\), \(y_\alpha\) the recorded datum, and \(n_\alpha\) additive disturbance.
The channel law is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.operational-measurement-model}
y_\alpha
=
\mathcal{M}_\alpha[\mathcal{F}]
+n_\alpha.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.operational-measurement-model} is the first operational
pillar: no observable is read from symbolic fields directly; it is inferred through
channel transduction physics plus disturbance structure \cite{stratton1941}.

Collect \(N\) channel outputs in
\(\mathbf{y}=[y_1,\dots,y_N]^\mathsf{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\), and let
\(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}\) denote estimator for target observable
\(\mathcal{O}[\mathcal{F}]\). Reconstruction map \(\mathcal{R}\) is accepted only if
\(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}=\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{y}]\) and
\(\lim_{\|n\|\rightarrow 0}\widehat{\mathcal{O}}=\mathcal{O}[\mathcal{F}]\).

In practice, identification quality is measured by local sensitivity. Let
\(\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^p\) parameterize admissible states, with Jacobian
\(\mathbf{J}_{\alpha i}=\partial \mathcal{M}_\alpha/\partial \theta_i\), and let
\(\mathbf{W}\succ 0\) be channel-weight matrix. Define weighted information matrix
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.operational-information-matrix}
\mathbf{G}
=
\mathbf{J}^\mathsf{H}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{J},
\end{equation}
where \((\cdot)^\mathsf{H}\) denotes Hermitian transpose. A poorly conditioned
\(\mathbf{G}\) indicates ill-posed inversion and large estimator variance
amplification; a well-conditioned \(\mathbf{G}\) indicates robust local inversion.

Uncertainty in reported observable is summarized by first-order propagation:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.operational-uncertainty-budget}
\sigma_{\mathcal{O}}^2
\approx
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{O}^{\mathsf{T}}
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\theta}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{O}
+\sigma_{\mathrm{cal}}^2
+\sigma_{\mathrm{model}}^2,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\theta}\) is parameter covariance induced by channel noise,
\(\sigma_{\mathrm{cal}}^2\) is calibration contribution, and
\(\sigma_{\mathrm{model}}^2\) is model-mismatch contribution \cite{stratton1941}.

Gauge and frame $\mathcal{D}_J=1$ must hold at the channel level, not only at symbolic
field level. For rotated state \(\mathcal{F}'=\mathcal{A}_R\mathcal{F}\) and
gauge-related potentials \((\mathbf{A}',\phi')\), each channel must satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.channel-covariance}
\mathcal{M}_\alpha[\mathcal{F}']
=
\mathcal{T}_\alpha(R)\,\mathcal{M}_\alpha[\mathcal{F}],
\qquad
\mathcal{M}_\alpha[\mathbf{A}',\phi']
=
\mathcal{M}_\alpha[\mathbf{A},\phi],
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{T}_\alpha(R)\) is known channel transformation law.

Calibration completion provides a direct test. Let
\(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{ref}}_\alpha\) be reference response and
\(\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_\alpha\) calibrated model used in inversion. Define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.channel-calibration-residual}
\rho_{\alpha}
=
\frac{\left\|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_\alpha-\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{ref}}_\alpha\right\|}
 {\left\|\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{ref}}_\alpha\right\|+\epsilon_{\alpha}},
\qquad
\rho_{\max}=\max_{\alpha}\rho_\alpha,
\end{equation}
with \(\epsilon_\alpha>0\) regularization.

An operational report is accepted only when the operational measurement specification in
Table~\ref{tab:ch1.admissibility-corollaries} is satisfied:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.observable-report-contract}
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{op}}=1
\iff
\Big(
\mathcal{D}_{h}=1,\
\kappa(\mathbf{G})\le \kappa_{\max},\
\rho_{\max}\le \rho_{\max}^{\mathrm{tol}},\
\sigma_{\mathcal{O}}\le \sigma_{\max}
\Big),
\end{equation}
where \(\kappa(\mathbf{G})\) is condition number of
\(\mathbf{G}\), \(\kappa_{\max}\) conditioning tolerance,
\(\rho_{\max}^{\mathrm{tol}}\) calibration tolerance, and \(\sigma_{\max}\)
uncertainty tolerance \cite{stratton1941}.

For torque channels used later, the measurement-facing integral form becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.operational-torque-channel}
\tau_z^{\mathrm{meas}}
=
\int_S\left(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{t}\right)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{z}}\,dS
+\delta\tau_z,
\end{equation}
where \(S\) is sensing surface, \(\mathbf{r}\) position relative to declared origin,
\(\mathbf{t}\) traction vector from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.traction-rotation-covariance},
\(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\) measurement axis, and \(\delta\tau_z\) net channel disturbance.

To compare setups with different torque scales, define normalized channel
reliability index
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.operational-torque-reliability}
\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}
=
\frac{\left|\tau_z^{\mathrm{meas}}-\tau_z^{\mathrm{model}}\right|}
 {\left|\tau_z^{\mathrm{model}}\right|+\epsilon_{\tau}},
\end{equation}
where \(\tau_z^{\mathrm{model}}\) is model prediction under calibrated channel model
and \(\epsilon_\tau>0\) regularization \cite{beth1936}.

Operational definition is therefore fixed in rigorous terms: an observable is a
symmetry-admissible field quantity equipped with identifiable channel operator, stable
reconstruction, calibrated completion, and explicit uncertainty budget. The following
subsection uses this foundation to show why local spin-orbit decompositions remain
interpretive tools unless they satisfy the same operational criteria.

\phantomsection\label{sec:ch01.4.4}
\subsection{Limits of local spin-orbit decompositions}
\label{sec:ch01.4.5}

% HARRINGTON-OPEN:sec:ch01.4.5
A spiral phase plate in front of a horn feed on a compact range defines the hardware scene for `Limits of local spin-orbit decompositions'': technicians compare turntable cuts and wattmeter totals on $\Gamma_R$ before paraxial labels are quoted \cite{allen1992}. Maxwell's equations on the declared exterior domain remain the governing constraint \cite{maxwell1873}.

Near-field micrographs invite a seductive partition: call polarization circulation
``spin'' and azimuthal phase gradient ``orbit'' at every pixel. Maxwell theory
permits such pictures only as convention-tagged diagnostics: the total density
\(\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\) does not decompose uniquely into
\((\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{loc}},\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}})\) unless gauge, boundary
data, split family, and measurement kernel are declared together
\cite{stratton1941}. The limits below are constructive---local splits
remain valuable when their equivalence class and completion tests are explicit.

Local spin-orbit decomposition remains mathematically valuable but physically
delicate: two admissible gauge splittings may assign different local ``spin-like'' and
``orbital-like'' patterns while preserving identical total angular-momentum transfer.
The limits established here are constructive rather than prohibitive: local splits
serve as interpretation tools, and they are promoted to direct observables only
when gauge, boundary, and channel-level completion are satisfied. Decomposition is
not forbidden; the task is to state precisely when decomposition outputs carry the
same epistemic status as stress-flux integrals and when they remain
convention-tagged diagnostics attached to a fixed total \cite{stratton1941}.

Canonical split families (equivalence class viewpoint). Two split families appear repeatedly in the literature and differ even when
\(\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\) is held fixed. \emph{Family~A} assigns orbital content
to a cross-product structure \(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}}\propto\mathbf{r}\times(\varepsilon_0\E)\)
built from potentials or fields, and spin content to a complementary bilinear term
in \(\E\) and \(\H\). \emph{Family~B} shifts content using a superpotential
\(\mathbf{C}\) so that \(\divg\mathbf{C}\) redistributes labels without altering
\(\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\). Neither family is ``wrong'' in isolation; each defines
an admissible representative of the same decomposition class. Physical disagreement
arises only when two groups report Family~A and Family~B reports as if they were
directly comparable observables without stating convention or passing the completion
criteria developed below \cite{belinfante1940}.

The Belinfante--Rosenfeld construction \cite{belinfante1940} explains the
mathematical origin of Family~B: symmetrizing the canonical energy-momentum tensor
requires adding a divergence term built from spin current, which reappears in local
angular-momentum splits as the superpotential \(\mathbf{C}\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.local-spin-orbit-split}. In Maxwellian electrodynamics the
practical consequence is strict: local spin-orbit images are gauge- and
convention-dependent coordinates on a conserved total
\cite{stratton1941}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.belinfante-symmetric-tensor}
\mathbf{T}^{\mathrm{sym}}
=
\mathbf{T}
+\frac{1}{2\jj\omega}\,\divg\mathbf{K},
\qquad
K_{ij}=\varepsilon_0\left(E_i H_j^*-E_i^* H_j\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{T}$ is the Maxwell stress tensor of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-stress}.
For radiation, $\mathbf{T}^{\mathrm{sym}}$ yields conserved momentum density and symmetric traction;
for measurement, torque integrals built from $\mathbf{T}^{\mathrm{sym}}$ align with Belinfante--Rosenfeld spin-flux completion \cite{belinfante1940}.
Belinfante \cite{belinfante1940} derived the symmetric energy-momentum tensor by
requiring that the total angular-momentum density
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.belinfante-total-am-density}
\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{tot}}
=
\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{sym}}
+\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{Bel}}
\end{equation}
obey \(\partial_t\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{tot}}+\divg\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{sym}}
=-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{ext}}\) with symmetric \(\mathbf{T}^{\mathrm{sym}}\) from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.belinfante-symmetric-tensor} and conserved integrated momentum.
Here \(\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{sym}}\) is momentum density from the symmetric tensor,
and \(\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{Bel}}\) is the spatial Belinfante spin density. Maxwell's
original stress-based torque law \cite{maxwell1873} controls the symmetric branch;
the canonical potential branch differs by the spin-flux divergence in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.belinfante-symmetric-tensor}. Family~B splits are
Belinfante-class representatives in which spin-labeled content is concentrated in
\(\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{Bel}}\) and orbit-labeled content in
\(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{sym}}\), modulo superpotential \(\mathbf{C}\)
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.local-spin-orbit-split}.

For monochromatic Maxwell fields in vacuum phasors,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.belinfante-spin-density}
\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{Bel}}
=
\frac{1}{2\jj\omega}\,\E^*\times\E
\;+\;
\frac{\mu_0}{2\jj\omega}\,\H^*\times\H,
\end{equation}
up to total-divergence terms movable into \(\mathbf{C}\). Equation
\eqref{eq:ch1.belinfante-spin-density} is gauge-invariant because it depends only on
\(\E,\H\). Orbital-labeled density
\(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{Bel}}=\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}-\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{Bel}}\)
then splits \(\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\) into gauge-invariant summands, unlike
potential-weighted decompositions that shift under
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.spin-orbit-gauge-redistribution}. Non-uniqueness persists because
any redistribution satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.spin-orbit-equivalence-class} produces
an equally valid local picture \cite{belinfante1940}.

Let \(\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}(\mathbf{r})\) denote electromagnetic
angular-momentum density already fixed in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.angular-momentum-balance}. A generic local decomposition takes
the form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.local-spin-orbit-split}
\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}
=
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}}
\;+\;
\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}
\;+\;
\divg\mathbf{C},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}}\) is orbital-labeled local density,
\(\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}\) is spin-labeled local density, \(\mathbf{C}\) is a
second-rank superpotential tensor, and \(\divg\mathbf{C}\) is the divergence
correction generated by decomposition convention. Equation
\eqref{eq:ch1.local-spin-orbit-split} makes clear that label assignment is not
unique at point level: superpotential terms can move content between
\(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}}\) and \(\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}\) while preserving
\(\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\). Local decomposition does not define a
single physical density pair; it defines an admissible equivalence class.

To represent this class explicitly, let
\((\mathbf{l}_a,\mathbf{s}_a,\mathbf{C}_a)\) and
\((\mathbf{l}_b,\mathbf{s}_b,\mathbf{C}_b)\) denote two admissible local splits of
the same \(\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\). Their difference can always be written
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.spin-orbit-equivalence-class}
\mathbf{l}_b-\mathbf{l}_a
=
\divg\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{q},
\qquad
\mathbf{s}_b-\mathbf{s}_a
=
-\divg\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{q},
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is redistribution tensor and \(\mathbf{q}\) is residual
balancing vector. The paired signs guarantee that local relabeling does not alter
the summed quantity \(\mathbf{l}+\mathbf{s}\), but they do alter each local
component separately.

Under a gauge update \((\mathbf{A},\phi)\to(\mathbf{A}',\phi')\) from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.gauge-transform}, the local pair transforms as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.spin-orbit-gauge-redistribution}
\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}'
=
\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}+\divg\mathbf{U}_\chi+\mathbf{v}_\chi,\qquad
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}}'
=
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}}-\divg\mathbf{U}_\chi-\mathbf{v}_\chi,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{U}_\chi\) is gauge-generated redistribution tensor and
\(\mathbf{v}_\chi\) is residual redistribution vector. The paired signs enforce
that \(\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}'+\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}}'
=\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}+\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}}\), but individual local
components remain gauge-sensitive. In functional terms,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.spin-orbit-gauge-redistribution} moves between admissible local
representatives; in physical terms, it explains why local spin-orbit decompositions can
differ without contradicting total transport predictions.

Integrating over a finite volume \(V\) with boundary \(\partial V\) and outward
unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) yields
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.spin-orbit-volume-test}
\int_V\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}'+\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}}'\right)\!dV
=
\int_V\!\left(\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}+\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{loc}}\right)\!dV
=
\int_V\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{em}}\,dV
\;-\;
\int_{\partial V}\mathbf{C}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS.
\end{equation}

Experimental spin torque versus local spin density. Beth \cite{beth1936} measured mechanical torque on a quartz wave plate illuminated

\subsection{Torque, momentum flux, and stress measurements}
\label{sec:ch01.5.2}

Measurement closes the loop opened in Subsections~\ref{sec:ch01.1.4} and~\ref{sec:ch01.1.5}: stress and angular flux are field-theoretic objects first, transducer readings second \cite{beth1936}. Beth's quartz plate did not read a modal index; it read mechanical torque from electromagnetic stress \cite{beth1936}. Before comparing $y_\tau$ on a Beth plate at $f=10\,\mathrm{GHz}$ with $\oint_{\Gamma_R}\mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS$ on a sphere at $R=3\,\mathrm{m}$, the experimenter must declare origin, surface orientation, polarization basis, harmonic gauge, and receiver noise floor---the same consistency checks any compact-range team publishes with a gain calibration report \cite{hansen1988}. The present development follows that
experimental logic: \(\mathbf{T}\) is the field-theoretic object inherited from
Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.1.4}; \(\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is traction on a
declared surface; \(y_\tau\) is what a calibrated transducer reports after geometry,
origin, and noise are specified. Momentum-flux and torque channels are therefore
specializations of one Maxwellian ladder, not independent postulates
\cite{poynting1884}.

The experimental lineage of torque readouts begins with Beth's 1936 detection of
mechanical rotation in quartz wave plates illuminated by circularly polarized light
\cite{beth1936}. That experiment established that polarization-linked angular
momentum has mechanical consequences at laboratory scale, decades before
Laguerre--Gaussian beams were identified as orbital-indexed modes
\cite{allen1992}. Modern torsion-balance protocols inherit the same logical
structure Beth used: predict torque from field stress, measure mechanical response,
and treat discrepancy as channel or regime error rather than as permission to
abandon conservation \cite{stratton1941}. Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.6.1}
returns to this completion pattern in fully worked form.

Let \(\mathbf{T}\) be Maxwell stress tensor from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-stress}, let \(S_m\) be the sensing surface around a
test body, and let \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) denote outward surface normal on \(S_m\).
The traction density is \(\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\), the
mechanical counterpart of Poynting's energy-flux concept \cite{poynting1884} applied
to linear momentum rather than power. For a
declared reference origin \(\mathbf{r}_0\), measured force vector
\(\mathbf{y}_F\), and measured torque component \(y_\tau\) about axis unit vector
\(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_\tau\), a channel-calibrated measurement model is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.stress-measurement-model}
\mathbf{y}_F
=\mathbf{K}_F\!\left(\int_{S_m}\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS\right)
+\mathbf{n}_F,\qquad
y_\tau
=K_\tau\!\left(
\int_{S_m}\!\big[(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)\times(\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\big]
\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_\tau\,dS
\right)+n_\tau,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{K}_F\) and \(K_\tau\) are calibration operators/scalars, and
\(\mathbf{n}_F,n_\tau\) collect channel disturbances. The two surface integrals are
deterministic electromagnetic load predictions inherited from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.maxwell-stress}; calibration and disturbance terms convert that
prediction into practical measurement channels.

For full vector torque reporting, define an explicit channel
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.stress-torque-vector-channel}
\mathbf{y}_T
=
\mathbf{K}_T\!\left(
\int_{S_m}\!\big[(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)\times(\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\big]\,dS
\right)+\mathbf{n}_T,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{K}_T\) is vector torque calibration operator and \(\mathbf{n}_T\) is
channel disturbance. The scalar channel \(y_\tau\) is then
\(y_\tau=\mathbf{y}_T\cdot\hat{\mathbf{u}}_\tau\) after consistent axis
normalization.

Origin declaration controls comparability of torque channels. If origin changes
from \(\mathbf{r}_0\) to \(\mathbf{r}_0'=\mathbf{r}_0+\Delta\mathbf{r}\), the
deterministic torque integral satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.stress-origin-shift-torque}
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{det}}'
=
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{det}}-\Delta\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{det}},
\end{equation}
with
\(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{det}}=\int_{S_m}\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS\) and
\(\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{det}}=\int_{S_m}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)\times(\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\,dS\).
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-origin-shift-torque} is the operational form of
origin dependence needed for laboratory-to-laboratory torque reconciliation.

Stress-based torque metrology layers calibration gain $K_\tau$, declared origin $\mathbf{r}_0$, and axis $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_\tau$ on top of the deterministic load from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-measurement-model} \cite{beth1936}. An origin shift $\mathbf{r}_0'=\mathbf{r}_0+\Delta\mathbf{r}$ rotates the torque vector by $-\Delta\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{det}}$ even when \((\E,\H)\) is unchanged---two groups comparing $y_\tau$ must match origins before debating polarization content \cite{stratton1941}.

Torque metrology is unforgiving of origin shifts \cite{beth1936}. On a torsion fixture with $\mathbf{r}_0$ at the plate center, moving the origin by $\Delta\mathbf{r}=2\,\mathrm{mm}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ while holding $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{det}}$ fixed injects a $z$-torque proportional to $F_y$ when $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_\tau=\hat{\mathbf{z}}$---the content of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-origin-shift-torque}, $\mathbf{T}'_{\mathrm{det}}=\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{det}}-\Delta\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{det}}$ \cite{stratton1941}. What goes wrong in the laboratory is comparing torque reports computed in different origin conventions without translating through this shift law \cite{harrington2001}.

At mathematical level, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-measurement-model} composes
field-to-traction operator mapping, surface integration, and instrument transfer in one
statement. At physical level, it explains why two laboratories can report
different torque values from the same field realization unless origin declaration,
axis definition, and calibration normalization are matched.

Surface selection is itself a measurable limit. For two admissible enclosing
surfaces \(S_a\) and \(S_b\), define stress-flux completion residual
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.stress-surface-closure-residual}
\mathcal{R}_S
=
\frac{
\left\|\int_{S_a}\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS-\int_{S_b}\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS\right\|
+\ell_\tau^{-1}\left\|\int_{S_a}\mathbf{r}\times(\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\,dS-\int_{S_b}\mathbf{r}\times(\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\,dS\right\|
}{
\left\|\int_{S_a}\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS\right\|
+\ell_\tau^{-1}\left\|\int_{S_a}\mathbf{r}\times(\mathbf{T}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\,dS\right\|
+\epsilon_S
},
\end{equation}
where \(\ell_\tau>0\) is scaling length and \(\epsilon_S>0\) is regularization.
Small \(\mathcal{R}_S\) indicates robust stress-channel completion across declared
sampling surfaces.

First-order torque uncertainty combines calibration, geometry, and noise:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.stress-torque-uncertainty}
\sigma_\tau^2
\approx
\left\|\frac{\partial y_\tau}{\partial K_\tau}\right\|^2\sigma_{K_\tau}^2
+\left\|\frac{\partial y_\tau}{\partial \mathbf{r}_0}\right\|^2\sigma_{r_0}^2
+\left\|\frac{\partial y_\tau}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{u}}_\tau}\right\|^2\sigma_u^2
+\sigma_{n_\tau}^2.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-torque-uncertainty} makes explicit that geometric
registration accuracy is as important as electrical calibration in torque
measurement quality.

Stress-based reports are accepted in this chapter only when interaction and channel
criteria hold simultaneously:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.stress-measurement-admissibility}
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}=1
\iff
\Big(
\mathcal{D}_m=1,\
\mathcal{R}_{S}\le R_{S,\max},\
\sigma_\tau\le \sigma_{\tau,\max},\
\|\mathbf{n}_F\|\le n_{F,\max}
\Big),
\end{equation}
where \(R_{S,\max}\), \(\sigma_{\tau,\max}\), and \(n_{F,\max}\) are declared
tolerances. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-measurement-admissibility} excludes
stress-channel claims with unresolved surface inconsistency or uncontrolled
uncertainty.

Stress-derived force and torque outputs are operationally admissible observables in

Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-measurement-model} defines calibrated stress channels.
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-origin-shift-torque} and
\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-surface-closure-residual} enforce geometric and surface
closure. Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-torque-uncertainty} enforces uncertainty

is necessary and sufficient for admissible reporting.

The model assumes quasi-static sensor response over one cycle-average window and
surface geometry known to sufficient precision for traction integration. Large
vibration bandwidth, unknown moving boundaries, or unresolved near-contact fields
can invalidate the simple calibration form in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.stress-measurement-model} and require dynamic inverse
identification \cite{stratton1941}.

\subsection{Modal projections and measurement limits}
\label{sec:ch01.5.3}

% HARRINGTON-OPEN:sec:ch01.5.3
Modal projections on finite probes recover only part of the angular spectrum \cite{stratton1941}. Maxwell's equations on the declared exterior domain remain the governing constraint \cite{maxwell1873}.

A spherical-harmonic index printed on a plot is rarely a direct observation; it is
almost always the output of a truncated fit to finite aperture data
\cite{hansen1988}. Modal projections are estimates of expansion
coefficients; measurement limits are the aperture, bandwidth, and noise constraints
that make those estimates ill-conditioned rather than exact. Until the projection
kernel is declared, a mode label remains inference under finite rank and calibration limits \cite{hansen1988}.

A sixty-probe scan on $\Gamma_R$ returns a finite vector of samples; a harmonic fit returns coefficients $\widehat{c}_{\ell m}$ subject to aperture rank and noise \cite{hansen1988}. The index printed on a contour plot is therefore conditional on \((N,\varepsilon,\mathcal{C})\)---not a direct wattmeter reading \cite{stratton1941}. Ill-conditioned inverses amplify labels that Maxwell export on fixed geometry cannot support \cite{chu1948}.

A spherical fit with $N=32$ probes and a chart to $\ell\le8$ may return non-zero $\widehat{c}_{8,m}$ even when the source export rank remains dipole-class \cite{hansen1988}. The fit resolves a truncated basis on the scan sphere; realizability and rank bounds come from feed geometry and outgoing selection, not from probe count alone \cite{stratton1941}. Treating high-$\ell$ fit coefficients as new multipole physics without a $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{real}}$ check is a common index confusion on compact ranges \cite{harrington2001}.

The informational character of accessibility appears with special clarity in modal
recovery. A spherical or cylindrical harmonic basis may list dozens or hundreds of
angular degrees of freedom, yet a finite scan surface, finite bandwidth, finite
dynamic range, and finite calibration budget jointly determine how many of those
directions enter the stabilized inverse problem below. The field state may contain
organized angular content that the measurement operator cannot separate from noise or
from coupling to neighbouring channels; that is the physical tragedy of $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{acc}}^{(N)}$
in operational form, not a comment on chart quality. Hansen's spherical
near-field theory \cite{hansen1988} and Harrington's source formulations
\cite{harrington2001} both encounter this wall from different sides---one from the
receiver, one from the transmitter---when effective angular dimension is counted on finite supports \cite{hansen1988}.

Modal language enters angular electrodynamics early because spherical and
cylindrical harmonics organize radiation patterns efficiently. Allen \emph{et al.}
\cite{allen1992} demonstrated that Laguerre--Gaussian modes carry a well-defined
orbital index and that mode converters transfer that index reversibly; yet a mode
label is not, by itself, an observable. 

The discussion connects backward to directional transport
(Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.2.1}) and forward to the observable-versus-inference
split (Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.5.4}). Directional flux \(\Pi(\theta,\phi)\) is
primary data on a sphere; modal coefficients are estimates obtained by inversion.
When inversion is ill-conditioned, modal interpretation must be tagged as
inference-only even if \(\mathcal{D}_J=1\) at the flux level. Section~\ref{sec:ch01.6}
returns to this distinction when multimode torque sums are tested against
measurement residuals.

Angular interpretation often proceeds through modal coefficients, but those
coefficients are not primitive observables; they are reconstructions from finite
channel data. In strict operational language, a \emph{modal projection} is a
channel-weighted linear functional on measured boundary data, and a \emph{measurement
limit} is any bound that prevents stable recovery of a modal degree of freedom from
available channels. This distinction is essential: a mode can be mathematically
definable yet experimentally unresolved. 

Let \(\Gamma_m\) denote a measurement surface, let
\(\{\mathbf{w}_q(\mathbf{r})\}_{q=1}^{Q}\) be known channel weighting functions on
\(\Gamma_m\), and let \(y_q\) be measured channel data. For tangential electric
field \(\E_t\), channel outputs satisfy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.modal-projection-data}
y_q=\int_{\Gamma_m}\mathbf{w}_q^*(\mathbf{r})\cdot\E_t(\mathbf{r})\,dS+n_q,
\qquad q=1,\dots,Q,
\end{equation}
where \(n_q\) is measurement noise in channel \(q\). Each integral is a weighted
projection of \(\E_t\) onto channel kernel \(\mathbf{w}_q\), so the data vector
\(\mathbf{y}\) captures only the subspace interrogated by the chosen sensing set.

Represent \(\E_t\) in a model basis \(\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_m\}_{m=1}^{M}\) with
coefficients \(a_m\): \(\E_t\approx\sum_{m=1}^{M}a_m\boldsymbol{\psi}_m\). Then
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.modal-projection-data} becomes the finite-dimensional inverse
system \(\mathbf{y}=G\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{n}\), where
\(G_{qm}=\int_{\Gamma_m}\mathbf{w}_q^*\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_m\,dS\). Define also
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.modal-projection-matrix}
G_{qm}
=
\int_{\Gamma_m}\mathbf{w}_q^*(\mathbf{r})\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_m(\mathbf{r})\,dS,
\end{equation}
and the channel information matrix
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.modal-information-matrix}
\mathbf{F}_{a}=G^*\Sigma_n^{-1}G,
\end{equation}
with \(\Sigma_n=\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf{n}]\). Small eigenvalues of
\(\mathbf{F}_a\) identify weakly observable modal directions.

A stable regularized estimator is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.modal-regularized-estimator}
\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_\lambda
=\left(G^*G+\lambda I\right)^{-1}G^*\mathbf{y},
\end{equation}
where \(G^*\) is conjugate transpose, \(I\) is identity matrix, and \(\lambda>0\)
is regularization parameter. Operator-wise, \(G^*G\) encodes channel sensitivity
to basis directions, \(\lambda I\) suppresses small-singular-value amplification,
and left multiplication by \((G^*G+\lambda I)^{-1}G^*\) yields the reconstructed
coefficient vector.

Using singular value decomposition \(G=U\Sigma V^*\), reconstruction acquires
spectral-filter form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.modal-spectral-filter}
\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_\lambda
=
\sum_{k=1}^{r}
\frac{\sigma_k}{\sigma_k^2+\lambda}
\left(\mathbf{u}_k^*\mathbf{y}\right)\mathbf{v}_k,
\end{equation}
where \(r=\mathrm{rank}(G)\), and \((\mathbf{u}_k,\mathbf{v}_k,\sigma_k)\) are
singular triplets. The filter factor
\(\sigma_k/(\sigma_k^2+\lambda)\) suppresses weakly measured directions.

Bias--variance tradeoff for this estimator is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.modal-bias-variance}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_\lambda-\mathbf{a}\right]
=
-\lambda\left(G^*G+\lambda I\right)^{-1}\mathbf{a},
\qquad
\mathrm{Cov}\!\left[\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_\lambda\right]
=
\left(G^*G+\lambda I\right)^{-1}G^*\Sigma_nG\left(G^*G+\lambda I\right)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Larger \(\lambda\) lowers variance at the price of increased modal bias; smaller
\(\lambda\) reduces bias but raises sensitivity to channel noise.

As a complete inverse relation,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.modal-regularized-estimator} records a central measurement
fact: modal descriptions are model-dependent estimates whose reliability depends
on channel geometry and conditioning, not on phase pattern alone. This condition
is the section-level safeguard against over-interpreting visually appealing but
weakly observable modal decompositions \cite{stratton1941}.

Quantitative resolution is bounded by singular-spectrum decay of \(G\). Let
\(\sigma_{\min}^{(\mathrm{eff})}\) be the smallest retained singular value after
truncation. Then, for perturbation \(\delta\mathbf{y}\), one obtains the
conditioning bound
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.modal-conditioning-bound}
\|\delta\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_\lambda\|_2
\le
\left\|\left(G^*G+\lambda I\right)^{-1}G^*\right\|_2\,
\|\delta\mathbf{y}\|_2
\equiv
\kappa_\lambda\|\delta\mathbf{y}\|_2.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.modal-conditioning-bound} defines amplification factor
\(\kappa_\lambda\), the primary conditioning index for modal reconstruction quality.

Effective observable modal dimension at threshold \(\tau_\sigma\in(0,1)\) is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.modal-effective-rank}
r_{\mathrm{eff}}(\tau_\sigma)
=
\#\left\{k:\sigma_k/\sigma_1\ge\tau_\sigma\right\}.
\end{equation}
The value \(r_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is the number of modal directions that remain
resolvable above the chosen singular-value floor.

To quantify aperture dependence, let
\(\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_\lambda^{(a)}\) and
\(\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_\lambda^{(b)}\) be reconstructions from two admissible
measurement surfaces \(\Gamma_a,\Gamma_b\). Define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.modal-cross-aperture-consistency}
\mathcal{C}_{ab}
=
\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_\lambda^{(a)}-\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_\lambda^{(b)}\|_2}
{\|\widehat{\mathbf{a}}_\lambda^{(a)}\|_2+\epsilon_a},
\qquad \epsilon_a>0.
\end{equation}
Small \(\mathcal{C}_{ab}\) indicates modal interpretation robust to admissible
aperture variation; large \(\mathcal{C}_{ab}\) indicates geometry-contingent modal
labels.

\subsection{Observables versus inferred quantities}
\label{sec:ch01.5.4}

% HARRINGTON-OPEN:sec:ch01.5.4
A standard-gain horn on a compact-range turntable fixes the laboratory scene for `Observables versus inferred quantities'': frequency, probe standoff, and wattmeter closure on $\Gamma_R$ precede the formal displays below \cite{hansen1988}. Maxwell's equations on the declared exterior domain remain the governing constraint \cite{maxwell1873}.

The measurement stack of Section~\ref{sec:ch01.5} would remain incomplete without
an explicit vocabulary for reporting status. Two quantities may share the same
symbol on a plot yet belong to different epistemic classes: one read directly from
a calibrated channel, the other recovered only after inversion with regularization
and model choice. Beth \cite{beth1936} measured torque directly; Allen \emph{et al.}
\cite{allen1992} inferred modal content from beam profiles---both legitimate, but not
interchangeable without the distinctions developed below
\cite{stratton1941}.

Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.5.4} closes the measurement discussion by resolving that
ambiguity: which reported quantities are direct observables and which are
inference-dependent descriptors. The distinction is mandatory for Chapter~\ref{ch:01},
because a mathematically coherent descriptor can still fail measurement admissibility
if its regime tag is omitted \cite{stratton1941}.

Let \(\mathcal{O}_d\) denote a direct observable produced by calibrated channel operator
\(\mathcal{M}\), and \(\mathcal{Q}_i\) denote an inferred quantity produced by
reconstruction operator \(\mathcal{R}\) from data \(\mathbf{y}\). The operational role
split is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.direct-inferred-role-split}
\mathcal{O}_d=\mathcal{M}[\mathcal{F}],\qquad
\mathcal{Q}_i=\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{y}],\qquad
\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{M}[\mathcal{F}]+\mathbf{n}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.direct-inferred-role-split} separates channel-output space
from reconstruction space: direct observables are channel-native, inferred
quantities are estimator-native.

To compare reliability classes on one scale, define inference $\mathcal{D}_J=1$ index
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.observable-inference-criterion}
\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{Q}_i)
=
\frac{\mathrm{Std}[\mathcal{Q}_i]}{\sigma_d}
\cdot
\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}
\cdot
\chi_{\mathrm{sym}},
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma_d\) is direct-channel uncertainty floor,
\(\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}\) inversion-sensitivity gain, and
\(\chi_{\mathrm{sym}}\ge 1\) symmetry-constraint penalty factor derived from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.channel-covariance}. The index rises when estimator uncertainty,
conditioning burden, or symmetry mismatch increases.

Because reconstruction reliability also depends on model discrepancy, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.inference-model-mismatch-index}
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{mis}}
=
\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathcal{M}[\widehat{\mathcal{F}}]\|_2}
{\|\mathbf{y}\|_2+\epsilon_m},
\qquad \epsilon_m>0,
\end{equation}
where \(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}\) is reconstructed field consistent with
\(\mathcal{Q}_i\). Small \(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{mis}}\) indicates channel-data
compatibility; large \(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{mis}}\) indicates unresolved model
error.

The reportability decision is stated by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.observable-decision-rule}
\begin{aligned}
\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{Q}_i)\le \tau_A
\ \wedge\ 
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{mis}}\le M_{\max}
&\Longrightarrow
\text{report as constrained observable surrogate},\\
\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{Q}_i)> \tau_A
\ \vee\ 
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{mis}}> M_{\max}
&\Longrightarrow
\text{report as inference-only descriptor}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here \(\tau_A>0\) and \(M_{\max}>0\) are protocol thresholds fixed before analysis.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.observable-decision-rule} converts qualitative caution into
an explicit reporting rule.

At chapter level, direct-observable status requires all upstream criteria:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.global-observable-admissibility}
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}=1
\iff
\Big(
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}=1,\
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}=1,\
\mathfrak{A}(\mathcal{Q}_i)\le \tau_A,\
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{mis}}\le M_{\max}
\Big).
\end{equation}
This equation joins channel calibration, modal conditioning, and inference
regime distinction in one acceptance statement.

\label{sec:ch01.6.2}

Superposed $m=+2$ and $m=-2$ beams illustrate channel cancellation: azimuthal intensity may show four lobes while net $\oint \mathbf{M}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,dS$ vanishes when amplitudes match \cite{belinfante1940}. Two coherent circularly polarized beams of equal power illuminate a flat observation
aperture \(S_o\) at \(f=10\,\mathrm{GHz}\). Beam~\(+\) carries azimuthal index
\(m=+2\); beam~\(-\) carries \(m=-2\). Both share radial envelope
\(A(\rho)=A_0 e^{-\rho^2/\rho_0^2}\) with \(\rho_0=5\,\mathrm{mm}\). A phase camera
records a four-lobed azimuthal intensity pattern. A torsion sensor on the source
mount reports torque about \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\). Using the channel-balance law from
Section~\ref{sec:ch01.2} and stress channels from Section~\ref{sec:ch01.5}, determine
whether the visible phase structure implies net angular-momentum transport, and
quantify the imbalance sensitivity if one channel is five percent stronger than the
other .

The experiment isolates a logical error common in angular-momentum discourse:
confusing \emph{phase richness} with \emph{flux imbalance}. Counter-rotating channels
of equal intensity produce standing azimuthal texture---nodes, lobes, and strong
\(\partial_\phi E\)---while their signed angular-momentum contributions cancel on
integration. Allen \emph{et al.} \cite{allen1992} showed that a \emph{single}
helical mode exports definite orbital flux; the present superposition of \(+m\) and
\(-m\) channels with equal weight deliberately removes that export while retaining
visible azimuthal structure. The problem therefore tests necessity: transport requires
\(I_+-I_-\neq 0\), not merely \(m\neq 0\) in the field exponent
\cite{allen1992}.

The schematic organizes the following blocks: \emph{aperture block} (gray, top) fixes \(S_o\) and \(\rho_0\); the \emph{\(+m\) channel
block} (blue) and \emph{\(-m\) channel block} (red) are equal counter-rotating ingress
carriers; the \emph{interference block} (purple) is the standing azimuthal lobe pattern;
the \emph{balance block} (gray, bottom) states the zero-transport verdict and imbalance
test.

The solution proceeds in four steps. \emph{Superposition on the aperture.}
Form \(E_t=E_++E_-\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-channel-amplitudes} with equal
amplitudes \(A_+=A_-=A(\rho)\). The result is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-counter-rotating-superposition}.
\emph{Interference reading:} two counter-rotating carriers synthesize a standing
azimuthal lobe pattern; local circulation exists but with opposite sign.

\emph{Per-channel power accounting.}
Compute \(I_\pm=|A_\pm|^2\). Under equal-amplitude condition,
\(I_+=I_-=|A(\rho)|^2\) everywhere on \(S_o\).
\emph{Balance check:} each helical ingress carries half the total power; neither
channel dominates the angular budget.

\emph{Net flux from channel difference.}
Substitute into Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-net-am-transport}:
\(\dot J_z=(m/\omega)\int_{S_o}(I_+-I_-)\,dS\).
\emph{Transport rule:} only \(I_+-I_-\) enters net export; matched powers cancel
regardless of azimuthal index \(m\).

\emph{Predicted torque on the mount.}
Apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-zero-torque-prediction}:
\(\tau_z^{\mathrm{pred}}=-\dot J_z=0\).
\emph{Null-readout interpretation:} zero torque is the expected Maxwellian outcome
for balanced channels, not a failed sensor.

\emph{Five-percent imbalance test.}
Introduce \(I_+=1.05\,I_0\), \(I_-=0.95\,I_0\) and evaluate
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-imbalance-sensitivity}.
\emph{Sensitivity lesson:} minute loss or ellipticity mismatch is enough to convert
a vivid phase image into weak but nonzero transport.

Let a monochromatic transverse field on observation surface \(S_o\) be formed by
superposing two coherent counter-rotating helical channels with common radial
envelope \(A(\rho)\), cylindrical coordinates \((\rho,\phi)\), and integer order
\(m>0\):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex62-counter-rotating-superposition}
E_t(\rho,\phi)
=
A(\rho)e^{\jj m\phi}+A(\rho)e^{-\jj m\phi}
=
2A(\rho)\cos(m\phi).
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-counter-rotating-superposition} displays strong angular
patterning through lobe/nodal structure of \(\cos(m\phi)\), so any purely geometric
inspection would classify the field as richly phase-structured.

Complete step-by-step derivation. Start from left- and right-circular components on \(S_o\):
\(E_+ = A(\rho)e^{\jj m\phi}/\sqrt{2}\) and
\(E_- = A(\rho)e^{-\jj m\phi}/\sqrt{2}\), each normalized so
\(|E_\pm|^2 = |A(\rho)|^2/2\). Superposition gives
\(E_t = \sqrt{2}A(\rho)\cos(m\phi)\) as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-counter-rotating-superposition}. The intensity difference is
\(I_+ - I_- = |E_+|^2 - |E_-|^2 = 0\) at every \(\rho\) when amplitudes match.

For each helical channel, the time-averaged angular-momentum flux through \(S_o\)
scales as \((\pm m/\omega)\) times channel power
\(P_\pm = \int_{S_o} I_\pm\,dS\) \cite{stratton1941}. Summing channels,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex62-channel-sum}
\dot J_z
=
\frac{m}{\omega}(P_+ - P_-)
=
\frac{m}{\omega}\int_{S_o}(I_+-I_-)\,dS,
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-net-am-transport}. When \(I_+=I_-\), both \(P_+\) and
\(P_-\) are equal and \(\dot J_z=0\) even though \(|E_t|^2 = 2|A|^2\cos^2(m\phi)\)
exhibits \(2m\) azimuthal nodes per cycle.

The Poynting vector for each circular component rotates in opposite senses; their
sum produces a standing azimuthal pattern in \(|E_t|\) without net azimuthal power
flow \cite{poynting1884}. Stress-flux integration confirms
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-zero-torque-prediction}: mechanical torque vanishes because
exported angular momentum vanishes. Any experimental nonzero torque under equal
channels implicates \(I_+\neq I_-\) from loss, ellipticity mismatch, or aperture
truncation breaking the symmetry assumed in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-equal-channel-condition} \cite{stratton1941}.

To make the transport test explicit, write the two-channel complex amplitudes as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex62-channel-amplitudes}
E_{+}(\rho,\phi)=A_+(\rho)e^{\jj m\phi},\qquad
E_{-}(\rho,\phi)=A_-(\rho)e^{-\jj m\phi},
\end{equation}
with intensities \(I_+(\rho)=|A_+(\rho)|^2\) and \(I_-(\rho)=|A_-(\rho)|^2\). Let
\(\dot J_z\) be net angular-momentum transport rate through \(S_o\). The inherited
channel-balance law gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex62-net-am-transport}
\dot J_z
=
\frac{m}{\omega}
\int_{S_o}\!\big(I_+-I_-\big)\,dS.
\end{equation}
Here \(m/\omega\) is channel angular-momentum-per-energy factor and \(I_+-I_-\) is
signed transport imbalance. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-net-am-transport} therefore
states that net transport is controlled by channel imbalance, not by visual phase
richness.

The equal-amplitude counterexample is the special case
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex62-equal-channel-condition}
I_+(\rho)=I_-(\rho)\quad\forall \rho\in S_o.
\end{equation}
Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-net-am-transport} yields
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex62-zero-transport-result}
\dot J_z=0
\qquad\text{while}\qquad
E_t(\rho,\phi)=2A(\rho)\cos(m\phi)\neq 0.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-zero-transport-result} is the central negative result of
visible phase structure is not sufficient for net
angular-momentum transport.

To connect this with measurable torque channels, use the stress-to-torque mapping
from Section~\ref{sec:ch01.5}. In steady operation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex62-zero-torque-prediction}
\tau_z^{\mathrm{pred}}
=
-\dot J_z
=
0
\quad\text{under Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-equal-channel-condition}}.
\end{equation}
Hence a nonzero torque measurement in this counterexample signals either channel
imbalance, asymmetry in measurement sequence, or unresolved environmental coupling.

A small imbalance perturbation provides first-order sensitivity. Let
\(I_+(\rho)=I_0(\rho)+\delta I(\rho)\) and
\(I_-(\rho)=I_0(\rho)-\delta I(\rho)\). Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex62-imbalance-sensitivity}
\dot J_z
=
\frac{2m}{\omega}\int_{S_o}\delta I(\rho)\,dS.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-imbalance-sensitivity} quantifies how small channel
biases convert phase-structured but balanced fields into weak net-transport states.

To separate pattern visibility from transport capability, define phase-structure
index and transport index
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex62-visibility-transport-indices}
\mathcal{V}_{\phi}
=
\frac{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|\partial_{\phi}E_t(\rho_*,\phi)\right|^2\,d\phi}
{\int_{0}^{2\pi}|E_t(\rho_*,\phi)|^2\,d\phi+\epsilon_v},
\qquad
\mathcal{T}_{J}
=
\frac{|\dot J_z|}{P_{\mathrm{tot}}/\omega+\epsilon_t},
\end{equation}
where \(\rho_*\) is representative radius, \(P_{\mathrm{tot}}\) total channel
power, and \(\epsilon_v,\epsilon_t>0\) regularizations. In the equal-channel
counterexample, \(\mathcal{V}_{\phi}\) can be large while \(\mathcal{T}_{J}=0\).
This explicitly demonstrates necessity-versus-sufficiency separation.

diagnostic statement: visual twisting or lobe count cannot by itself establish
mechanical torque capability. A transport claim requires signed channel imbalance
or equivalent flux criterion, exactly as enforced by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-net-am-transport}.

The worked-case admissibility criterion is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex62-admissibility-gate}
\mathcal{D}_{62}=1
\iff
\Big(
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mod}}=1,\
\left|\int_{S_o}(I_+-I_-)\,dS\right|\le \Delta I_{\max},\
|y_{\tau}|\le y_{\tau,\max},\
\mathcal{T}_{J}\le T_{\max}
\Big),
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta I_{\max}\), \(y_{\tau,\max}\), and \(T_{\max}\) are declared
thresholds. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex62-admissibility-gate} ensures that the
counterexample is accepted only when channel balance and measurement evidence are
consistent.

\subsection{Gauge dependence of local angular-momentum densities}
\label{sec:ch01.6.3}

Gauge relabeling leaves $(\E,\H)$ fixed while reshuffling information between scalar

Consider a finite spherical control volume \(V_g\) of radius \(R=2\lambda\) encloses a
center-fed dipole at \(f=1\,\mathrm{GHz}\). Two potential representations are
available: Coulomb gauge \((\mathbf{A}_C,\phi_C)\) and Lorenz gauge
\((\mathbf{A}_L,\phi_L)\), both producing identical \(\E,\H\) on \(\partial V_g\).
A post-processing script may compute a local angular-momentum density

Any local density built from potentials alone can therefore
exhibit gauge-orbit motion even when integrated flux observables are fixed. The
problem requires a reporting discipline: distinguish \emph{diagnostic maps} (may vary
with gauge) from \emph{observable integrals} (must pass contamination criteria). This
is the laboratory counterpart of the gauge discussion in Section~\ref{sec:ch01.4}
\cite{stratton1941}.

The schematic organizes the following blocks: \emph{volume block} (gray, centre) is the control region \(V_g\); the \emph{boundary-field

The solution proceeds in four steps. \emph{Gauge orbit on potentials.}
Relate \((\mathbf{A}',\phi')\) to \((\mathbf{A},\phi)\) via
\(\mathbf{A}'=\mathbf{A}+\nabla\chi\), \(\phi'=\phi-\jj\omega\chi\), with
\(\chi\) inherited from Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.4.2}.
\emph{Invariance check:} \(\chi\) relabels potentials only; \(\E\) and \(\H\) must
remain unchanged.

\emph{Pointwise density shift.}
Define \(\delta_\chi\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{loc}}=\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{loc}}'-\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{loc}}\).
\emph{Diagnostic warning:} local colour plots can disagree strongly even when fields match;
the density is gauge-tagged, not gauge-neutral.

\emph{Volume divergence accounting.}
Apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex63-gauge-density-balance} to decompose
\(\int_{V_g}\delta_\chi\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{loc}}\,dV\) into boundary flux and
residual source channels.
\emph{Partition rule:} gauge variation splits into relocatable boundary terms and
genuine volumetric contamination.

\emph{Integrated report comparison.}
Evaluate \(\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{int}}\) and \(\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{int}}'\) from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex63-integrated-report} and compute
\(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{int}}\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex63-integral-contamination-index}.
\emph{Observability test:} integral contamination, not local hue, controls promotion
to observable status.

\emph{Epistemic criterion.}
Accept gauge-sensitive local patterns as observables only if
\(\mathcal{D}_{63}=1\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex63-observable-criterion}; otherwise report
them as inference-only intermediates.
\emph{Reporting discipline:} explicit labeling is required before publication-grade
claims.

Take a finite control volume \(V_g\) with boundary \(\partial V_g\), outward unit
normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\), and two gauge-related potential representatives
\((\mathbf{A},\phi)\) and \((\mathbf{A}',\phi')\). The gauge-variation structure is
inherited from Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.4.2}; no new gauge theorem is introduced.
The worked problem is to quantify when local angular-momentum decompositions disagree while
integrated transport observables remain gauge-robust.

Let \(\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{loc}}\) be a candidate local angular-momentum density
constructed from potentials and fields, and define gauge difference
\(\delta_\chi\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{loc}}

Applying the inherited structure from Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.4.2} gives
\begin{equation}

\int_{V_g}\delta_\chi\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{loc}}\,dV

\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{\Xi}_\chi\) is gauge-induced flux tensor and \(\mathbf{\Psi}_\chi\)

redistribution, while the second is volumetric contamination.

\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

standalone observables.

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}

\end{equation}

\begin{equation}

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{int}}
=
\frac{\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{int}}'-\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{int}}\|}
{\|\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{int}}\|+\epsilon_c},
\qquad \epsilon_c>0.
\end{equation}

\end{equation}

\subsection{Reactive versus radiative comparison}
\label{sec:ch01.6.4}

qualified torque only on a radiative shell \cite{stratton1941}. The two-shell protocol

Consider a center-fed \(\lambda/2\) dipole radiates at \(f=1\,\mathrm{GHz}\). Two concentric

\(y_{P,k}\), reactive power \(y_{Q,k}\), and torque magnitude \(y_{\tau,k}\) about
\(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\). Inner shell \(kr_1=0.5\) samples reactive storage where
\(|y_{Q,1}|\) may dominate \(|y_{P,1}|\); outer shell \(kr_2=5\) samples radiative
export where Poynting flux and torque channels align with $\mathcal{C}_3$
\cite{stratton1941}. Comparing $y_{\tau,1}$ to $y_{\tau,2}$ without regime
classification mixes storage physics with transport claims. Using regime indices from
Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.2.4}, classify each shell as storage-diagnostic or
export-diagnostic, compute
\(\mathcal{R}_Q(r_k)\), \(\mathcal{C}_\tau\), and gated efficiency
\(\eta_\tau(r_k)\), with \(\mathcal{D}_{64}=1\)
\cite{stratton1941}.

The two-shell protocol mirrors what happens when one laboratory measures ``angular
momentum'' in the reactive near zone and another measures torque in the far zone.
At $1\,\mathrm{GHz}$ on a $\lambda/2$ dipole, only the outer shell at $kr=5$ carries
the export-diagnostic label appropriate for watt-calibrated transport reports; the inner
shell at $kr=0.5$ remains in the reactive regime treated in
Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.2.4} \cite{stratton1941}. Both may record structured phase and polarization, yet Maxwellian interpretation
differs: inner-shell data primarily diagnose oscillatory storage exchange; outer-shell
data test export-compatible torque channels. Without explicit \(\mathcal{R}_Q\)
comparison, apparent disagreement is often a regime error, not a physics conflict
\cite{stratton1941}.

The schematic organizes the following blocks: \emph{source block} (gray) is the centre-fed dipole; the \emph{inner-shell block}

The solution proceeds in five steps. \emph{Shell power functionals.}
Evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex64-power-functionals} on \(S_{r_1}\) and \(S_{r_2}\).
\emph{Regime vocabulary:} \(P(r)\) measures irreversible export; \(Q(r)\) measures
oscillatory storage---the reactive-radiative split of Section~\ref{sec:ch01.2.4}.

\emph{Reactive-ratio distinction.}
Compute \(\mathcal{R}_Q(r_k)=|Q(r_k)|/(|P(r_k)|+\epsilon_P)\) from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex64-reactive-ratio}.
\emph{Shell reading:} \(\mathcal{R}_Q\gg 1\) marks reactive dominance where phase
can mislead; \(\mathcal{R}_Q\ll 1\) supports export interpretation.

\emph{Torque contrast across shells.}
Form \(\mathcal{C}_\tau=|\tau_z(r_2)|/(|\tau_z(r_1)|+\epsilon_\tau)\) from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex64-torque-contrast}.
\emph{Transport emergence:} large outer-to-inner contrast shows torque channels
activating only beyond the reactive shell.

\emph{Gated efficiency correction.}
Apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex64-gated-efficiency} with factor
\(1/(1+\mathcal{R}_Q)\) to suppress false efficiency in reactive zones.
\emph{Interpretation guard:} raw torque-to-power ratios overstate transport when
\(|Q|\gg |P|\).

\emph{Trend consistency and criterion.}
Evaluate \(\mathcal{T}_{12}\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex64-trend-consistency} and
confirm \(\mathcal{D}_{64}=1\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch1.ex64-regime-admissibility}.
\emph{Joint verdict:} both shells must occupy expected classes and the
\(r_1\to r_2\) trend must be internally consistent.

Let \(S_{r_1}\) and \(S_{r_2}\) be concentric spherical observation surfaces,
with radii \(r_1<r_2\), centered on the same source. The objective is to compare
angular-structure interpretation in a reactive-dominant surface \(S_{r_1}\) and a
radiative-dominant surface \(S_{r_2}\), using the regime logic inherited from
Subsection~\ref{sec:ch01.2.4}.

Consider a half-wave dipole at \(ka=1\) with observation shells at
\(kr_1=0.5\) and \(kr_2=5\), where \(k=\omega/c\). On \(S_{r_1}\), the reactive
integral \(Q(r_1)\) typically exceeds \(|P(r_1)|\), yielding
\(\mathcal{R}_Q(r_1)\gg 1\); phase winding and polarization ellipticity can be
large while exported power and axis torque remain small. On \(S_{r_2}\),
\(|P(r_2)|\gg |Q(r_2)|\) restores conservation-compatible transport
interpretation, and \(\mathcal{C}_\tau\gg 1\) signals that torque channels become
measurably active only after the reactive shell is crossed
\cite{stratton1941}. This two-surface protocol is the
measurement counterpart of the regime indices \(\chi\) and \(\chi_\Omega\) from
Section~\ref{sec:ch01.2.4}.

Define active-power and reactive-power surface functionals
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex64-power-functionals}
P(r)=\int_{S_r}\Re\!\left\{\mathbf{S}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\right\}dS,\qquad
Q(r)=\int_{S_r}\Im\!\left\{\mathbf{S}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\right\}dS,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{S}=\tfrac{1}{2}\E\times\H^*\) is the complex Poynting vector
\cite{poynting1884} and \(\Im\{\cdot\}\) extracts imaginary part. The first integral
measures net exported power; the second measures reactive circulation associated
with local storage exchange.

To compare regimes on one normalized axis, define reactive ratio
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex64-reactive-ratio}
\mathcal{R}_Q(r)=\frac{|Q(r)|}{|P(r)|+\epsilon_P},
\qquad \epsilon_P>0.
\end{equation}
Reactive-dominant behavior corresponds to \(\mathcal{R}_Q\gg 1\), while
radiative-dominant behavior corresponds to \(\mathcal{R}_Q\ll 1\). This scalar
ratio makes regime distinction operationally testable at each observation
surface.

For a torque channel about \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\), define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex64-torque-contrast}
\mathcal{C}_\tau
=
\frac{|\tau_z(r_2)|}{|\tau_z(r_1)|+\epsilon_\tau},
\qquad
\epsilon_\tau>0.
\end{equation}
The numerator and denominator are measured torque magnitudes on outer and inner
surfaces; \(\epsilon_\tau\) is a small regularization constant preventing division
singularity. When \(Q(r_1)\gg P(r_1)\) and \(P(r_2)\gg |Q(r_2)|\), one typically
obtains \(\mathcal{C}_\tau\gg 1\): near-zone angular structure is visually rich
yet weakly transport-capable, while far-zone structure carries measurable torque.

Measurement channels at the two surfaces are written
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex64-surface-channel-model}
y_{P,k}=K_{P,k}P(r_k)+n_{P,k},\qquad
y_{Q,k}=K_{Q,k}Q(r_k)+n_{Q,k},\qquad
y_{\tau,k}=K_{\tau,k}\tau_z(r_k)+n_{\tau,k},
\end{equation}
for \(k\in\{1,2\}\), where \(K_{\bullet,k}\) are calibration factors and
\(n_{\bullet,k}\) are channel disturbances. Equation
\eqref{eq:ch1.ex64-surface-channel-model} ensures that regime claims are tied to
calibrated outputs rather than inferred from qualitative field appearance.

Define regime-gated transport efficiency
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex64-gated-efficiency}
\eta_{\tau}(r_k)
=
\frac{|\tau_z(r_k)|}{P(r_k)/\omega+\epsilon_\eta}
\cdot
\frac{1}{1+\mathcal{R}_Q(r_k)},
\qquad \epsilon_\eta>0.
\end{equation}
The factor \(1/(1+\mathcal{R}_Q)\) suppresses apparent efficiency in reactive
regimes where local circulation dominates exported transport.

Surface-to-surface trend consistency is captured by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ch1.ex64-trend-consistency}
\mathcal{T}_{12}
=
\frac{\left|\eta_{\tau}(r_2)-\eta_{\tau}(r_1)\right|}
 {\eta_{\tau}(r_2)+\epsilon_t},
\qquad \epsilon_t>0.
\end{equation}
Large \(\mathcal{T}_{12}\) indicates strong regime transition in transport
effectiveness, which is expected when \(r_1\) is reactive-dominant and \(r_2\) is
radiative-dominant.
